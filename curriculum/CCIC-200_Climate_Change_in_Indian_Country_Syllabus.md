## Course Description

Climate change is not a distant forecast in Indian Country. It is already here: in rivers that run too warm for salmon in August, in smoke that settles over camp and classroom, in snow that falls as rain, in First Foods that ripen early or not at all. This course starts where understanding starts, with experience. It begins with a cloud, because everyone knows what one is, and works outward to the atmosphere, the carbon cycle, and the gauges and data points that tell a story of change over time, not on speculation but on momentum.

Climate is social as much as it is scientific. So the course also asks how people come to know what they know, whose knowledge they trust, and why Indigenous Knowledges and Western science see farther together than either sees alone. Students keep a weather journal, observe a place over weeks, sit with an Elder or knowledge holder and listen, read a real Tribal climate adaptation plan, analyze local climate trends, and draft the outline of an adaptation plan for a concern in their own community.

Sovereignty is the organizing principle. Climate work is sovereignty work: deciding what matters, what data to trust, who holds knowledge, and who decides. The lands, waters, and Beings who live within them are relatives, not resources. The Columbia River Basin, with its salmon, its Treaty Tribes, and its dams, is the shared case study; every student also centers their own Nation and homelands.

The surest sign of understanding is being able to pass it on. By the end, each student should be able to explain climate change so that both an Elder and a child understand, and carry a working plan outline forward into TCR 201, their community, and their career.

## Course Overview

This 10-week course examines climate change impacts, perceptions, and adaptation strategies in American Indian and Alaska Native communities, with a focus on developing practical climate adaptation planning skills. Through multiple ways of knowing, students explore how climate has changed in the past, how it is changing now, the contemporary causes of climate change, and what changes are to come. The course is designed for Tribal College and University students from many Nations: the Columbia River Basin serves as a shared case, and each student applies the learning to their own Tribe, homelands, and community.

## Course Learning Objectives

1. **Understand** weather and climate patterns affecting Tribal lands.
2. **Apply** local sensing techniques to document environmental changes.
3. **Analyze** climate data and projections relevant to Tribal Nations.
4. **Evaluate** climate hazards and vulnerabilities specific to Tribal communities.
5. **Understand** climate adaptation strategies for Tribal Nations.
6. **Connect** with knowledge holders and practitioners engaged in climate adaptation in your Tribe or community.

## Outcomes (NWIC Catalog)

Students who complete the course will be able to:

1. **Apply** basic concepts of meteorology, climatology, and Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) to explain and understand their local climate and environment.
2. **Summarize** how Earth's climate system works, and understand the physical processes and dynamic interactions of the atmosphere, oceans, ice, and land surface.
3. **Describe** changes in climate through time, and distinguish between long-term geologic-scale climate change and recent human-caused climate change.
4. **Discuss** current impacts of climate change on humans and ecosystems, with emphasis on impacts to Coast Salish Tribes and other Native Peoples. `[Instructor note: outside NWIC, name the Tribes of your region alongside Coast Salish Tribes, for example Columbia River Treaty Tribes.]`
5. **Illustrate** specific examples of climate change impacts on plants, animals, land, or water resources in your Tribal community.
6. **Discuss** Peoples' unique adaptations to their place, and demonstrate examples of how TEK and intergenerational knowledge of the natural world can assist in solving environmental issues.
7. **Describe** various approaches to collecting and analyzing data, including field data collection, visual data analysis, and using data to understand trends or discover underlying issues.
8. **Apply** scientific knowledge and TEK toward positive solutions to the impacts of climate change while respecting Tribal values and strengthening community ties.
9. **Describe** the global carbon cycle and the role of carbon dioxide (CO2) in creating a greenhouse effect, including the recent (100-year) changes in the composition of the atmosphere and its role in global warming.

## Course Design

### Modular Design: Presence, Application, Inquiry, and Reflection

Every week moves through the same four modules from NESC 215. Students always know the shape of the time together.

- **Presence:** showing up for each other. Opening circle, check-in, going outside, a guest or Elder when one is available. Presence is where relationships are built, and it is graded as participation, not performance.
- **Application (Assignments):** doing the work with hands, instruments, maps, and data. Every week has a hands-on activity.
- **Inquiry:** asking questions that lead to more questions. Short presentations, readings, discussion, and the week's key lesson.
- **Reflection:** writing or speaking about what shifted. Each week has a reflection prompt carried from NESC 215.

### The Four-Part Spine: Understanding a Climate of Change

The four-part series by Patrick Freeland and Cayla Jones, "Understanding a Climate of Change: Understanding an Ever Changing Climate," gives the course its arc. Each part's key lesson and key takeaway appear in the weeks it anchors.

| PF Series Part | Core Idea | CCIC 200 Weeks |
|---|---|---|
| Part 1: How Does a Cloud Work? | Weather is physics; the same gauges that predict tomorrow show change over years. | Weeks 2 and 3 |
| Part 2: How Do You Know What You Know? | Climate is social as much as scientific; read your sources and yourself. | Weeks 4 and 6 |
| Part 3: Adaptation, Mitigation, Resilience | Three different jobs with different tools; the impacts are already here. | Weeks 7, 8, and 9 |
| Part 4: Relationships Matter | It takes all of us, including our non-human relatives; be a good ancestor. | Weeks 1, 4, and 10 |

Week 5 (Ecosystem Impacts) is the bridge between Part 2 and Part 3: it moves from how we know change to what change is doing to First Foods, plants, animals, and water.

### The Columbia River Basin as Shared Case, Your Homelands as Center

TCUs serve students from many Nations. The course uses one shared case so that everyone can reason together, and asks every student to carry each lesson home.

- **Shared case:** the Columbia River Basin: salmon, the four Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC) member Tribes (Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation (CTUIR), Confederated Tribes of the Warm Springs Reservation of Oregon, Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation, and Nez Perce Tribe), Treaty-reserved rights, dams, warming water, snowpack loss, and Tribal leadership in restoration and climate planning.
- **Your homelands:** in every assignment, students may center their own Tribe and place. A student from the Plains, the Southwest, Alaska, or the Great Lakes does the same work for home.
- **Boundaries:** students never present Treaty, ceded, or Usual and Accustomed boundaries as definitive jurisdiction without the Nation's own maps and words.

### Meeting Pattern Assumption

The lesson plans assume **one 2-hour (120-minute) session per week** on a 10-week quarter, matching NWIC. For **two 75-minute sessions per week** (150 minutes), use this split:

- **Session A (75 min):** Presence, Inquiry, and the first part of Application.
- **Session B (75 min):** the rest of Application, Reflection, and the extra 30 minutes for field time, guest conversation, or assignment studio.

Students should plan on about `[PLACEHOLDER: expected outside hours per credit at your institution]` hours of outside work per week, including the weather journal.

### Filling Weeks 7 and 8: The Design Choice

The NESC 215 source jumps from Week 6 to Week 9. CCIC 200 fills the gap this way:

- **Week 7: Climate Hazards and Risks in the Northwest.** Heat, drought, wildfire and smoke, flooding and atmospheric rivers, snowpack loss, and ocean change, with hazard cascades and the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module. This gives Week 9 (Risk, Impact, and Vulnerability) the hazard knowledge it needs.
- **Week 8: The Carbon Cycle, Climate Through Time, and Mitigation Science.** This week teaches NWIC Outcome 9 (the global carbon cycle and CO2), which no NESC week teaches explicitly, and strengthens Outcome 3 (geologic versus recent climate change). It also gives PF Part 3 its "mitigation" week, so Weeks 7 to 9 read as Adaptation context, Mitigation, and Resilience planning.

Social vulnerability, health, and First Foods are not dropped: First Foods anchor Week 5, and health and social vulnerability anchor Week 9.

## Assignments

Each assignment lets students center their own Tribe and homelands. Where an assignment involves Tribal knowledge, the data care rules in "Data Sovereignty and Data Care" apply.

### 1. Weather Journal (Weeks 2 to 9)

**Purpose:** to build the habit of observing weather as physics you can see and measure, and to notice how descriptive language tells a story.

**What to do:**
- Make at least five entries per week, at roughly the same time of day when you can.
- Each entry records: date, time, location; sky and cloud types; temperature (felt, and measured if you have a thermometer); wind (direction and strength in your own words); precipitation; and one thing living Beings are doing (birds, plants, insects, fish, people).
- Once a week, compare one entry with the nearest official record (National Weather Service station, USGS stream gauge, SNOTEL site, or a CoCoRaHS observer), found through the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module or the agency's site.
- If you carry words for weather in your own language and choose to use them, you may. No one is expected to, and no one should borrow another Nation's words without permission.

**Deliverable:** the journal (paper, sketchbook, or digital) and a one-page summary at Week 9: What patterns did you see? What surprised you? What did the official record miss, and what did you miss?

**Grading notes:** graded on consistency, attention, and growth in observation, not on artistic quality or spelling. Check-ins at Weeks 4 and 7 are feedback only. Sketches count as entries.

### 2. Field Observation Project (Assigned Week 3, Due Week 6)

**Purpose:** to combine local sensing with instrumental observation at one place over time.

**What to do:**
- Choose one site you can visit safely at least three times between Weeks 3 and 6: a creek, shoreline, garden, fishing site, schoolyard, or your own yard. If the site is culturally sensitive, do not record its exact location in anything you turn in.
- **Local sensing:** at each visit, spend ten quiet minutes using all your senses. Record sounds, smells, color, water level and clarity, plant stage, animal signs, and what people nearby say about the place.
- **Instrumental observation:** at each visit, take at least two measurements (air temperature, water temperature, rain gauge reading, pH strip, turbidity by eye or with a tube) and one fixed-point photograph from the same spot and angle.
- **Comparison:** pull the matching official record for your visit days (nearest weather station, USGS gauge, or SNOTEL) using the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module or agency sites.

**Deliverable:** field notes from all visits, the photo-point series, a simple data table, and a two-page summary: What did your senses tell you that the instruments did not? Where did they agree? What would you monitor next, and for how long?

**Grading notes:** 7 percent of the grade. Weighted toward the quality of observation and the honesty of the comparison. Missing or messy measurements are fine if explained. Optional extension: register as a CoCoRaHS observer and keep reporting after the course.

### 3. Listen and Learn: A Conversation With an Elder or Knowledge Holder (Assigned Week 4, Due Week 9)

**Purpose:** to learn from intergenerational knowledge about environmental change, and to practice doing that respectfully.

**What to do:**
1. **Find the right path in.** Ask through your family, your Tribe's cultural resources or education department, or the instructor. Some Tribes require approval before a student interviews an Elder for a class; follow your Tribe's protocols.
2. **Ask for consent before the conversation.** Explain who you are, why you are asking, what the class will see, and what it will not. Ask whether you may take notes or record. Make it clear the knowledge holder may decline any question, stop at any time, or withdraw everything later. Use the consent note in the appendix, or the Tribe's own form if it has one.
3. **Offer compensation or a gift.** Follow the knowledge holder's and the community's customs. The institution provides `[PLACEHOLDER: honorarium amount, gift budget, and how students request it]`. Students do not pay out of pocket.
4. **Listen.** Use the Story of Self, Story of Us, Story of Now prompts in the appendix as a guide, not a script. Ask about changes they have seen in weather, water, plants, animals, and seasons over their life, and what they would tell the next generation.
5. **Care for what you were given.** Treat everything shared as Tier 3 (sovereign) by default: it stays with you and the knowledge holder. Offer to share your notes back. Return or delete recordings as agreed.

**Deliverable:** a three- to four-page reflection on what you learned about change and about listening, written so it contains nothing the knowledge holder has not cleared for the class. Attach a signed or recorded consent note (kept by the instructor, not shared) and a short "what may be shared" note that says which parts, if any, the knowledge holder released for class discussion.

**Grading notes:** 8 percent of the grade. Graded on respect for process, quality of listening, and reflection, never on how much knowledge was "collected." A student who asks respectfully and is told "not now" has done the assignment well; they write about that experience and complete an alternative (a public talk by a Tribal knowledge holder, a Tribe-produced oral history, or an in-class Elder visit). Students never publish or post knowledge from this assignment.

### 4. Cultural Resource Impacts Review (Assigned Week 5, Due Week 7)

**Purpose:** to read a real Tribal climate adaptation plan (CAP) or vulnerability assessment the way a planner does, and see how a Tribe names its cultural resources and the climate threats to them.

**What to do:**
- Choose one public plan: Lummi Nation, CTUIR, Swinomish Indian Tribal Community, or your own Tribe's. Find plans through the Tribal Resilience Actions database (https://tribalresilienceactions.org/plans/), the Tribal Climate Change Guide (https://tribalclimateguide.uoregon.edu/), or the Tribe's own website. The CTUIR Climate Adaptation Plan executive summary is at https://ctuir.org/media/oeklm2el/cap-executive-summary-final-std-pg-no.pdf .
- Build a table: cultural resource named in the plan; climate hazard(s) linked to it; how the plan rates or describes vulnerability; strategies proposed; gaps you notice.
- Note how the plan frames its values (for example, CTUIR's First Foods order: water, salmon, deer, cous, huckleberry).

**Deliverable:** the table and a two- to three-page review: What does the plan protect first, and why? How does it bring together science and TEK? What is missing or unclear? What would you ask the plan's authors?

**Grading notes:** 7 percent. Use only what the plan publishes. Critique respectfully: a plan reflects a Nation's decisions, capacity, and moment in time. Students may not have access to non-public Tribal plans; they should not ask for them for a class assignment.

### 5. Local Climate Trend Analysis (Assigned Week 6, Due Week 7)

**Purpose:** to read historical climate records and downscaled projections for your homelands, and explain them plainly.

**What to do:**
- Use The Climate Toolbox (https://climatetoolbox.org/), including the Tribal Climate Tool, for one location in your homelands and one Columbia River Basin location (for example, near Celilo, Pendleton, Toppenish, or Lapwai).
- Pull at least three variables that matter to a concern you care about (for example: summer maximum temperature, number of very hot days, April 1 snow water equivalent, annual or seasonal precipitation, stream flow).
- Compare the historical baseline with mid-century projections under a lower and a higher emissions scenario.
- Use the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module (https://atniclimate.github.io/dynamic-drought-module/) to set this year's conditions (drought, streamflow, snowpack, heat) beside the long-term picture.

**Deliverable:** three labeled charts or screenshots with sources and dates, a two-page memo (what changed, what is projected, how certain, what it means for your concern), and one paragraph written so that both an Elder and a child could understand it.

**Grading notes:** 7 percent. Weighted toward correct reading of the charts, honest statements about uncertainty, and the plain-language paragraph.

### 6. Carbon Cycle Lab (In Class, Week 8)

**Purpose:** to describe the global carbon cycle and the recent change in atmospheric CO2 (NWIC Outcome 9).

**Deliverable:** a completed lab sheet with the Keeling curve plot and interpretation, a labeled carbon cycle diagram, and three sentences connecting it to one Tribal mitigation example. Details are in the Week 8 lesson plan.

**Grading notes:** 3 percent. Graded for completion and accuracy of the core ideas. Students who miss class complete it with the online NOAA data.

### 7. Framing Worksheet (Part 1 in Week 7, Part 2 in Week 8, Due Week 8)

**Purpose:** to scaffold the adaptation plan outline. This worksheet comes from American Indian Higher Education Consortium (AIHEC) climate resilience research mentorship with TCU student researchers.

**What to do:** first, name your issue (water, a First Food, crops, a road, a town, a river). Then fill in five columns:

| What's Known | What's Under Threat | Projected Impacts | Methods | Mitigation (and Adaptation) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Put your data here first: observations, measurements, maps, records, what knowledge holders shared and cleared. | Health, land, water, economy, community, ways of life, relatives. | What the projections say (from your Trend Analysis and regional sources). | How you would find out more: testing, monitoring, records, conversations. | What can be done to offset climate impacts? Sort each idea: adaptation (living with change) or mitigation (reducing the cause). |

- **Part 1 (Week 7):** What's Known, What's Under Threat, Projected Impacts.
- **Part 2 (Week 8):** Methods and Mitigation (and Adaptation).

**Deliverable:** the completed worksheet with sources noted.

**Grading notes:** 4 percent. A full, honest worksheet with gaps named is better than a polished one that hides them.

### 8. Climate Adaptation Plan Outline (Assigned Week 9, Due Start of Week 10)

**Purpose:** to turn the framing worksheet into the outline of an adaptation plan for one priority concern.

**Outline sections:**
1. **Community and place:** whose plan this would be; the rights holders, relatives, and partners; the place and its relatives.
2. **Priority concern and values:** the concern, and the values that make it a priority (for example, a First Food, a fishing site, drinking water, Elders' health in heat).
3. **Climate trends and projections:** from your Trend Analysis.
4. **Vulnerability:** exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity.
5. **Goals and candidate strategies:** each labeled adaptation or mitigation.
6. **Knowledge, data, and data care:** what is needed; who holds it; how it will be protected (TSDF tier).
7. **Engagement:** who must be at the table, and how (rights holder engagement, knowledge holders, Tribal departments, youth).
8. **Monitoring:** three to five indicators you would track.
9. **Next steps:** what TCR 201 would add (implementation, funding, leadership engagement).

**Deliverable:** a three- to five-page outline.

**Grading notes:** 4 percent. Graded for coherence from data to strategy, and for respect for community decision-making. Students are drafting, not deciding for a Tribe.

## Final Product Options

Students choose one of the following formats. In CCIC 200, a "completed" plan is a complete draft for one priority concern, built from the adaptation plan outline. TCR 201 carries it into implementation, funding, and leadership engagement.

1. **Comprehensive Report (Individual or Team)**
   - Completed Tribal climate adaptation plan (CAP) draft for one priority concern
   - Proposed implementation strategies
   - Timeline and resource requirements
   - Monitoring and evaluation framework

2. **Professional Presentation (Individual)**
   - PowerPoint or similar format
   - Visual representation of key findings from a Tribal CAP or your own plan outline
   - Implementation roadmap
   - Rights holder engagement plan

3. **Interactive Project (Individual)**
   - Community engagement or art
   - Educational materials
   - Implementation guidebook
   - Training materials

**For every option:** include a one-minute explanation that both an Elder and a child could understand, a source list, and a short data care statement (what Tribal knowledge is included, with whose permission, at what tier).

## Assessment Criteria

Final products are evaluated on:

- Understanding of climate science concepts
- Ethical integration of TEK and Indigenous Knowledge Systems (IKS)
- Practicality of proposed solutions
- Quality of analysis and presentation
- Grounded consideration of community needs and resources
- Feasibility of implementation strategies

## Final Product Rubric

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Exemplary (4) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Understanding of climate science concepts** | Key concepts missing or confused (weather and climate, greenhouse effect, projections). | Core concepts present but with errors or unexplained jargon. | Concepts accurate and connected to the concern; uncertainty stated. | Concepts accurate, clearly linked from global to local, and explained so an Elder and a child could follow. |
| **Ethical integration of TEK / IKS** | Tribal knowledge absent, generic, or used without permission. | Knowledge referenced but loosely, or without clear attribution or consent. | Knowledge included only with permission, attributed as agreed, and treated as a way of knowing, not decoration. | Knowledge and science are woven with care; consent, tier, and protocol are explicit; the product strengthens relationships. |
| **Practicality of proposed solutions** | Solutions vague or unrelated to the identified impacts. | Solutions related but general, or unclear whether adaptation or mitigation. | Solutions specific, matched to impacts, and labeled adaptation or mitigation. | Solutions specific, sequenced, and grounded in examples that have worked in Tribal communities. |
| **Quality of analysis and presentation** | Little use of data; sources missing. | Data used, but charts unclear or conclusions overstated. | Data read correctly, sources cited, presentation organized and clear. | Data and observation combined persuasively; visuals clear; the story of change is easy to follow. |
| **Grounded consideration of community needs and resources** | Community absent, or described from the outside. | Community named, but needs and strengths assumed. | Needs, strengths, and existing capacity identified from real sources. | Community voice (with permission) shapes priorities; rights holders and relatives are central. |
| **Feasibility of implementation strategies** | No sense of who, when, or with what resources. | Some steps named; resources and roles unclear. | Realistic roles, timeline, resources, and indicators. | Realistic and adaptive: names risks, fallback options, and what TCR 201 or the Tribe would need to carry it forward. |

Final Product score = average of the six criteria, converted to 25 points.

### Week 2: Weather and Climate Foundations

**PF Series Thread:** Part 1, How Does a Cloud Work?

**Key lesson:** Weather is based on fundamental physics, here and throughout the universe. Pressure and water are always moving, never fixed or stuck at one height.

**Key takeaway:** The same physics, and the same gauges and data points that predict tomorrow's weather, also show us patterns and change over many years. With enough data we can look ahead to what may come, not on speculation but on momentum.

**Learning objectives:** Students will be able to:
- Distinguish weather (the state of the atmosphere now) from climate (weather averaged over about 30 years or more).
- Explain how pressure, water, and heat make a cloud, rain, and wind.
- Describe local weather and climate patterns through more than one way of knowing.
- Name at least two climate impacts already documented on Tribal lands.

**Before class:**
- NCA5 Chapter 27, "Northwest": Key Messages and introduction.
- Norton-Smith, K., et al. (2016). *Climate Change and Indigenous Peoples: A Synthesis of Current Impacts and Experiences.* USDA Forest Service General Technical Report PNW-GTR-944. Read the introduction.
- Spend ten minutes on earth.nullschool.net. Find your homelands. Watch the wind. Switch to total precipitable water.

**Agenda (120 minutes):**

| Time | Module | What Happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 15 min | Presence | Go outside if you can. Everyone looks up for three minutes and describes the sky in their own words. Return and share. |
| 15 to 45 min | Inquiry | Presentation: distinguishing weather from climate. What a cloud is: warm air rising, cooling, and condensing water vapor on particles; pressure differences driving wind; the atmosphere "dripping like an air conditioner for rain" and picking up speed for storms. The greenhouse effect as a preview for Week 8. |
| 45 to 85 min | Application | Cloud physics and earth.nullschool lab (see activity). |
| 85 to 105 min | Inquiry | Ways of knowing local weather and climate patterns; Indigenous action and perspectives on climate change; case studies of climate impacts on Tribal lands (Columbia River salmon and warm water; the student's own homelands). |
| 105 to 120 min | Reflection | Launch the weather journal. Introduce the reflection. |

**Hands-on activity: How Does a Cloud Work?**
1. **Cloud in a bottle:** a clear plastic bottle, a little warm water, and a small amount of smoke (or a hand pump). Squeeze and release: pressure drops, air cools, water vapor condenses on particles, and a cloud appears. Students write down what they saw before explaining it.
2. **earth.nullschool:** in pairs, students find a low-pressure system in the North Pacific and track wind and moisture toward the Columbia River Basin and toward their own homelands. Where is moisture coming from? Where is it going?
3. **From weather to climate:** show one day of temperature at a local station, then 30 years of the same date. Ask: what did one day tell us? What did 30 years tell us?

**Assignment: Weather Journal** (see Assignments). Begins now and runs through Week 9.

**Reflection (NESC 215):** Many words for rain… how does descriptive weather and climate tell a story?

**Guest or Elder slot:** `[PLACEHOLDER: meteorologist from the local National Weather Service forecast office, or Tribal air quality or environmental staff]`.

**Instructor notes:** Keep numbers honest: recent-decade global warming is about 1.1 to 1.3 °C (2.0 to 2.4 °F) above the 1850 to 1900 average. Do not repeat the Robinson draft's "33.8 °F" figure; see "Facts to Get Right."

### Week 3: Local Sensing and Environmental Observation

**PF Series Thread:** Part 1, How Does a Cloud Work? (the gauges and data points).

**Key lesson:** Observation is a skill. People have always sensed change in their places; instruments add a record that can be compared across places and years.

**Key takeaway:** Your senses and your instruments answer different questions. Put them side by side and each makes the other stronger.

- Compare a personal observation with an official record.

| Time | Module | What Happens |
|---|---|---|

### Week 4: Human Perceptions of Climate Change

**PF Series Thread:** Part 2, How Do You Know What You Know?, and Part 4, Relationships Matter.

### Week 5: Ecosystem Impacts

**PF Series Thread:** Bridge from Part 2 (how we know change) to Part 3 (responding to it).

**Key lesson:** Climate change reaches people through their relatives: the water, fish, animals, roots, and berries that sustain them. When a First Food is stressed, a way of life is stressed.

**Key takeaway:** Salmon are a measuring stick. Watching their water, their timing, and their returns tells us about the health of the whole system, including ourselves.

**Learning objectives:** Students will be able to:
- Explain how warming, changing flow, and changing seasons affect traditional foods, wildlife, plants, and water.
- Describe the Columbia River salmon case: historic abundance, dams, warm water, and the 2015 die-off.

- Optional: Kimmerer, *Braiding Sweetgrass*, "Burning Cascade Head."

| Time | Module | What Happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 min | Presence | Check-in: a food, plant, or animal that matters to your family, and its season. |
| 10 to 45 min | Inquiry | Climate effects on traditional foods; changes in wildlife patterns; plant community shifts; water resource impacts. Columbia River case (see instructor notes). |
| 45 to 95 min | Application | First Foods impact web (see activity). |

**Hands-on activity: First Foods Impact Web.**
1. Start with CTUIR's published First Foods order as an example: water, salmon, deer, cous, huckleberry. Note that the order itself carries meaning that CTUIR defines; do not interpret it for them.
2. Each student builds a web for one relative from their own homelands (a fish, plant, animal, or water source), using only what they know or what is publicly shared by their Tribe. **Instructor notes: Columbia River case points.**
- Historic returns are estimated at 10 to 16 million salmon and steelhead a year.
- The four CRITFC member Tribes reserved fishing rights at usual and accustomed places in their 1855 Treaties; the rights were reserved, not granted (*U.S. v. Winans*, 1905). See "Facts to Get Right" on the Robinson draft's 1846 Oregon Treaty error.
- Celilo Falls was flooded by The Dalles Dam on March 10, 1957.

### Week 6: Climate Projections

**PF Series Thread:** Part 2, How Do You Know What You Know?

**Key lesson:** Projections are not predictions of a single future. They are the physics of the climate system run forward under different choices, and they are only as useful as our ability to read them honestly.

**Key takeaway:** Data literacy is sovereignty. A Nation that can read its own climate data can decide for itself what matters, instead of waiting for someone else to tell it.

**Learning objectives:** Students will be able to:

### Week 7: Climate Hazards and Risks in the Northwest

**PF Series Thread:** Part 3, Adaptation, Mitigation, Resilience (adaptation context).

**Key lesson:** Adaptation, mitigation, and resilience are not the same. They are different jobs with different tools: policy, groundwork, preparation, data, knowledge, funding, and sometimes emergency. Adaptation starts with knowing the hazards already at the door.

**Key takeaway:** The impacts are already here, so we respond quickly and on purpose, without getting lost in fear, the future, and the "black mirror" of media.

**Learning objectives:** Students will be able to:
- Explain the main climate hazards in the Northwest: extreme heat, drought, wildfire and smoke, flooding and atmospheric rivers, snowpack loss, and ocean change (warming, marine heatwaves, acidification, sea level rise).
- Distinguish a hazard from a risk (risk depends on hazard, exposure, and vulnerability).
- Trace a hazard cascade (for example, fire, then rain, then debris flow, then sediment on salmon redds).
- Build a hazard profile for their homelands using live data.

**Before class:**
- NCA5 Chapter 27, "Northwest": sections on heat, wildfire, water, and ocean.
- Mote, P. W., Li, S., Lettenmaier, D. P., Xiao, M., and Engel, R. (2018). "Dramatic declines in snowpack in the western US." *npj Climate and Atmospheric Science* 1.
- U.S. Drought Monitor (https://droughtmonitor.unl.edu/): look at the current map for your state and read the map legend.

**Agenda (120 minutes):**

| Time | Module | What Happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 min | Presence | Check-in: which hazard has touched your family or community? Pass if you want. |
| 10 to 45 min | Inquiry | Hazard tour (see content notes). Hazard versus risk. The three jobs of Part 3. |
| 45 to 95 min | Application | Hazard profile and cascade map (see activity). |
| 95 to 110 min | Application | Framing Worksheet, Part 1: begin What's Known, What's Under Threat, and Projected Impacts for your issue. |
| 110 to 120 min | Reflection | Introduce the reflection. Journal check-in (feedback only). |

**Content notes for the hazard tour:**
- **Extreme heat:** the late June 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome set all-time records across the region. Warmer nights matter as much as hot days, because bodies, fish, and buildings do not cool down. The Robinson draft reports Nez Perce climate staff describing Tribal members without air conditioning, and swimming holes made unusable by harmful algal blooms.
- **Drought and snow drought:** in 2015, near-normal precipitation fell as rain instead of snow in much of the region, leaving low snowpack and low, warm summer flows. Snow drought is a warming signal.
- **Wildfire and smoke:** heat and drought lengthen fire seasons. Smoke travels far beyond the fire. The 2017 Eagle Creek Fire in the Columbia River Gorge is a regional example.
- **Flooding and atmospheric rivers:** long, narrow plumes of Pacific moisture bring much of the region's heavy rain. Rain on snow and rain on burned slopes raise flood and debris-flow risk.
- **Snowpack loss:** less snow and earlier melt shift water from summer, when fish, farms, and forests need it most, to winter and spring.
- **Ocean change:** warming, marine heatwaves, and acidification (the ocean absorbing CO2) stress shellfish and the salmon food web; sea level rise threatens coastal villages and sites.
- **Cascades:** the Robinson draft describes how, on Nez Perce homelands, fires are followed by floods and landslides that send sediment into streams and smother redds.

**Hands-on activity: Hazard Profile and Cascade Map.**
1. Open the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module with the Columbia and Snake Basin preset. Turn on layers for drought, heat, fire perimeters, streamflow, snowpack, and National Weather Service alerts. Click a reservation boundary to read its place-based impact briefing. Copy the view's shareable URL into your notes.
2. Repeat for your own homelands. Note which hazards are active now and which are seasonal.
3. Build a cascade map: pick one hazard and draw at least four linked consequences, ending at a relative (a First Food, a person, a place). Mark which links you know from data and which from experience.
4. Name one adaptation already happening in your community for that hazard (a cooling center, fuel reduction, a floodplain project, an alert system).

**Assignment: Framing Worksheet, Part 1** (see Assignments). Cultural Resource Impacts Review and Local Climate Trend Analysis are due this week.

**Reflection (new, in the NESC 215 style):** What is already here? Which of these hazards has your family or community already lived through, and what did people do?

**Guest or Elder slot:** `[PLACEHOLDER: Tribal emergency manager, wildland fire staff, or public health staff]`.

**Instructor notes:** The ATNI Dynamic Drought Module is free and public, with no login. Its Tribal lands and Treaty layers ship empty on purpose, so each Nation populates its own copy; do not fill them from outside sources in class. Verify any 2026 events from the Robinson draft before using them (see "Facts to Get Right").

### Week 8: The Carbon Cycle, Climate Through Time, and Mitigation Science

**PF Series Thread:** Part 3, Adaptation, Mitigation, Resilience (mitigation).

**Key lesson:** Carbon moves in a circle through air, ocean, land, and living Beings. Burning fossil carbon, stored underground for millions of years, adds carbon to the air faster than the circle can take it back, and that thickens the greenhouse blanket.

**Key takeaway:** We must mitigate now if we are to limit the impacts on future generations. Mitigation is the only job that reduces the cause, and Tribes are already doing it on their own terms.

**Learning objectives:** Students will be able to:
- Describe the global carbon cycle: its reservoirs (atmosphere, ocean, land and living things, fossil carbon) and the flows between them.
- Explain the greenhouse effect and the role of CO2.
- Read the Keeling curve and describe the change in atmospheric CO2 over the last 100 years and beyond.
- Distinguish long-term geologic climate change from recent human-caused change.
- Describe natural climate solutions and Tribal mitigation examples, and when clean energy conflicts with Tribal rights.

**Before class:**
- NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, "Trends in Atmospheric Carbon Dioxide" (https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends/): look at the Mauna Loa record and read the page.
- Fargione, J. E., et al. (2018). "Natural climate solutions for the United States." *Science Advances* 4(11): read the abstract and look at the main figure of pathways.
- NCA5 Chapter 3, "Earth Systems Processes": Key Messages.

**Agenda (120 minutes):**

| Time | Module | What Happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 min | Presence | Breathe out. Every exhale carries carbon that plants will take back in. Short check-in. |
| 10 to 40 min | Inquiry | The carbon cycle and greenhouse effect (see content notes). Climate through time. |
| 40 to 80 min | Application | Carbon Cycle Lab (see activity). |
| 80 to 100 min | Inquiry | Mitigation science: natural climate solutions; Tribal mitigation examples; clean energy and sacred places. |
| 100 to 112 min | Application | Framing Worksheet, Part 2: Methods and Mitigation (and Adaptation) columns. |
| 112 to 120 min | Reflection | Introduce the reflection. |

**Content notes:**
- **Greenhouse effect:** sunlight warms the surface; the surface gives off heat (infrared); CO2, water vapor, methane, and other gases absorb and re-emit part of that heat. Without any greenhouse effect, Earth's average surface temperature would be about minus 18 °C instead of about 15 °C. More CO2 means more heat held.
- **The recent change:** before industrialization, atmospheric CO2 was about 280 parts per million (ppm). Continuous measurement at Mauna Loa began in 1958. Recent annual values are above 420 ppm (check the current value on the NOAA page in class). Ice cores show CO2 stayed roughly between 180 and 300 ppm over the last 800,000 years.
- **Geologic versus recent change:** over tens of thousands of years, changes in Earth's orbit paced ice ages, with CO2 and temperature rising and falling together over thousands of years. The current rise has happened in about 150 years and tracks fossil fuel burning and land clearing.
- **The ocean:** the ocean has taken up a large share of human CO2 emissions and most of the excess heat. The first slows warming; both change ocean chemistry and life.
- **Natural climate solutions:** Fargione et al. (2018) estimated that 21 natural pathways (forests, grasslands, agricultural lands, and wetlands) could together offset roughly one fifth of U.S. net annual emissions. Many of these pathways overlap with Tribal land stewardship: forest management, cultural burning where Tribes lead it, and wetland and floodplain restoration.
- **Tribal mitigation examples (from the Robinson draft; verify details before teaching):** the Nez Perce Tribe's solar and storage, 72 electric vehicle chargers, and about 380,000 trees on 2,137 acres, estimated to reduce about 18,700 metric tons CO2 equivalent by 2030; CTUIR solar and battery storage at its food sovereignty center; Warm Springs transmission and solar work.
- **Clean energy and consent:** the proposed Goldendale pumped storage project sits near Pushpum, a site of great importance to the Yakama Nation. The Robinson draft reports a Washington Department of Ecology finding of "significant and unavoidable adverse impacts" on Yakama cultural resources; verify before classroom use. Frame for discussion: clean energy is needed; clean energy on sacred ground or without meaningful consultation is not climate justice.

**Hands-on activity: Carbon Cycle Lab.**
1. **Carbon accounts:** label four containers atmosphere, ocean, land and living things, and fossil carbon. Use beans or tokens. Run "years" where carbon moves naturally between the first three (photosynthesis, respiration, ocean exchange). Then add fossil carbon to the atmosphere each year. Students record what happens to the atmosphere's account.
2. **Keeling curve:** download or display the NOAA Mauna Loa monthly data. Students mark the 1958 value, a recent value, and pre-industrial 280 ppm. Why does the line zigzag each year? (Northern Hemisphere plants take in carbon in summer and release it in winter.)
3. **Connect:** write three sentences linking the lab to one Tribal mitigation example.

**Assignment:** Carbon Cycle Lab (in class). Framing Worksheet complete, due end of week.

**Reflection (new, in the NESC 215 style):** Carbon moves in circles. Where do you sit in that circle, and what part of it is yours to change?

**Guest or Elder slot:** `[PLACEHOLDER: Tribal energy, forestry, or climate program staff working on mitigation]`.

**Instructor notes:** Keep blame and individual guilt out of the center. Tribes have contributed little to the cause and are leading on the response. The mitigation question for this course is "what does mitigation look like when a Nation decides it?" Funding, policy, and project development for mitigation are TCR 201 topics.

### Week 9: Risk, Impact, and Vulnerability

**PF Series Thread:** Part 3, Adaptation, Mitigation, Resilience (resilience).

**Key lesson:** Climate impacts are happening now, so we must adapt now if we are to be resilient. Vulnerability is not a trait of a people; it comes from exposure, sensitivity, and the capacity a community has to respond, much of which history and policy have shaped.

**Key takeaway:** A plan is a set of relationships written down. Good adaptation planning starts with what a community values, uses the best data and knowledge it can trust, and names who decides.

**Learning objectives:** Students will be able to:
- Explain risk, impact, and vulnerability (exposure, sensitivity, adaptive capacity).
- Describe climate adaptation planning frameworks and Indigenous approaches to adaptation.
- Identify economic, health, and community considerations, including heat, smoke, and First Foods access.
- Describe rights holder engagement strategies.
- Draft an adaptation plan outline for one priority concern.

**Before class:**
- Whyte, K. P. (2013). "Justice forward: Tribes, climate adaptation and responsibility." *Climatic Change* 120.
- Tribal Climate Change Guide, University of Oregon (https://tribalclimateguide.uoregon.edu/): browse the adaptation plans section.
- Institute for Tribal Environmental Professionals (ITEP), Northern Arizona University, climate change adaptation planning resources (https://www7.nau.edu/itep/main/tcc/).

| 0 to 10 min | Presence | Check-in: one person or group you would want at the table for your concern. |
| 10 to 40 min | Inquiry | Understanding human risks, impacts, and vulnerabilities; climate adaptation planning frameworks; Indigenous approaches to adaptation; economic, health, and community considerations; rights holder engagement strategies. |

**Content notes:**
- **A common planning cycle:** scope the concern and values; gather data and knowledge; assess vulnerability; set goals; choose strategies; implement; monitor and adjust. Tribal plans adapt this cycle to their own governance and values.
- **Indigenous approaches:** plans organized around First Foods (CTUIR) or community-defined health indicators (the Swinomish Indian Tribal Community's work on Indigenous health indicators) put relationships and values at the center, not only infrastructure.
- **Health and social vulnerability:** heat and smoke hit Elders, children, outdoor workers, and people without cooling first; loss of First Foods affects health and culture together.
- **Rights holder engagement:** Tribal citizens are rights holders, not an interest group. Government-to-government consultation is a legal and relational duty. The Columbia River Treaty is a teaching example: Tribes and First Nations were excluded from the original 1964 Treaty; U.S. Tribes have participated in the review since 2012, and the Syilx Okanagan, Ktunaxa, and Secwepemc Nations were observers on the Canadian delegation. ## Course Policies

These policies are written for TCU students, many of whom are parents, workers, caregivers, fishers, harvesters, and active participants in ceremonial and community life. They assume that learning happens in relationship, and that a student's obligations to family and community are part of who they are, not an interruption.

### Presence and Attendance

- **Presence matters more than seat time.** Come when you can, and communicate when you cannot.
- **Family and ceremonial obligations** are legitimate absences. You do not need to explain ceremonial matters in detail; a short message is enough.
- **Seasonal harvest, hunting, gathering, and fishing** (including Treaty fishing seasons) are legitimate absences when you communicate before or soon after. Tell the instructor early in the term if you know your seasons.
- **Bereavement, funerals, and community emergencies** (fire, flood, smoke, evacuations) are excused.
- **Make-up path:** for any excused absence, complete a short make-up (read the lesson plan, do the activity where possible, and write the reflection). Presence is not reduced for excused absences.

### Deadlines and Flexibility

- Deadlines keep the class moving together; they are not traps. Ask for an extension before the deadline when you can, and after it when you cannot. Most requests will be granted.
- **Resubmission:** you may revise and resubmit any assignment (except the final) once, within two weeks of feedback.

### Skills-Based, Not Only Time-Based, Learning

TCU student researchers in AIHEC's climate resilience mentorship named the difference between time-based and skills-based learning, and found better outcomes with hands-on learning than with sitting and looking at a book. This course follows that lesson:

- Assignments are judged on what you can do and explain, not on hours logged.
- Every week includes hands-on work.
- If you already have a skill (for example, you have monitored water quality for your Tribe), talk to the instructor about showing it another way.
- If you are willing to fail at something, you will figure it out. Mistakes in fieldwork and data are expected and welcome when you explain them honestly.

Climate change carries grief, fear, and anger, especially when it touches home. Students may step out of a heavy discussion at any time. You may choose a different case study if a topic is too close. ## Working With Tribes and Knowledge Holders Respectfully

This course asks students and instructors to learn from Tribal Nations and knowledge holders. That is a privilege with responsibilities.

- **Consult the local Tribe.** Invite knowledge holders through the Tribe's education, cultural resources, or natural resources department, and follow the Tribe's protocols for guests, recording, and sharing.
- **Permission from some is not permission from all.** Permission from some Tribal leaders or one Tribe does not extend to others. When in doubt, ask again, and ask the right people.
- **Compensate knowledge holders.** Offer honoraria, gifts, travel, and meals according to local custom. Budget for this before the term starts.
- **Do not invent or borrow cultural content.** This document leaves Tribal teachings, stories, place names, and ceremonies to the Tribes. Instructors should too.
- **Treaty and ceded boundaries.** Do not present Treaty, ceded, or Usual and Accustomed boundaries as definitive jurisdiction without the Nation's own maps and words.
- **Treaty rights are reserved, not granted.** The Tribes retained rights they already held (*U.S. v. Winans*, 1905). *U.S. v. Washington* (1974, the Boldt decision) affirmed a 50 percent share of harvestable fish; *U.S. v. Oregon* (Belloni, 1969) continues through ongoing management agreements.
- **Label knowledge.** Local Contexts (https://localcontexts.org/) provides Traditional Knowledge (TK) and Biocultural (BC) Labels that Tribes use to mark how their knowledge may be used. Respect any label you see.
- **Follow CARE and OCAP** (see next section).
- **Names matter.** Use each Nation's own name for itself. Use the correct name "Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC)."
- **Tribes are governments.** Engagement with Tribal Nations is government-to-government; Tribal citizens are rights holders and relatives, and should be named that way.

## Data Sovereignty and Data Care

Data about Tribal Peoples, lands, waters, and relatives belongs under the authority of the Nations it comes from. Data literacy is sovereignty; so is data care.

- **CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance** (Global Indigenous Data Alliance): **C**ollective benefit, **A**uthority to control, **R**esponsibility, and **E**thics. See Carroll, S. R., et al. (2020), "The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance," *Data Science Journal* 19.
- **OCAP®** (First Nations Information Governance Centre): **O**wnership, **C**ontrol, **A**ccess, and **P**ossession of First Nations data by First Nations.
- **Tiered Sovereign Data Framework (TSDF):** ATNI's four-tier approach that puts CARE and OCAP into practice, using a watershed metaphor.

| Tier | Watershed Name | Meaning | In This Course |
|---|---|---|---|
| T0 | Rain | Open: released for public benefit by the Nation's own decision. | Public data (weather stations, gauges, published plans) and anything a Nation has chosen to publish. |
| T1 | Lake | Network: shared among Indigenous network members through reciprocal protocols. | Rarely used in class; only with the Nation's direction. |
| T2 | River | Negotiated: shared with outside partners through formal agreements. | Not used in class without a formal agreement. |
| T3 | Canoe | Sovereign: culturally sensitive; stays under the community's complete control. | The default for anything from a knowledge holder. It stays with the student and the knowledge holder. |

**Default rule:** if you are not sure which tier something belongs to, treat it as T3. Over-protecting can be corrected; under-protecting may cause harm that cannot be undone.

**Student practices:**
- Keep Listen and Learn notes and recordings private; return or delete them as agreed.
- Do not post, publish, or upload Tribal knowledge from this course, including to AI tools.
- Do not record exact locations of culturally sensitive sites in assignments.
- When you cite a knowledge holder, cite them only as they agreed.
- Respect people's fear of how data could reflect on them. A TCU student researcher learned that farmers declined to share soil results because they worried the data would reflect badly on them; participants need to know what you are doing, in a way they understand.

**ATNI Climate tools and data:** ATNI's tools support Tribal decisions; they never replace them. The Dynamic Drought Module is public (T0) and leaves Tribal lands and Treaty layers empty so each Nation fills its own copy. ATNI-GeoBase is a harmonized public baseline (T0) that Tribes layer their own data on and keep sovereign. Access to non-public tools: `[PLACEHOLDER: current access contact for ATNI Climate tools]`.

## TCU Student Research Examples

> **What TCU students have built.** These projects come from the 2024 AIHEC climate resilience summer research mentorship. They are described without names or personal details.
>
> - **River erosion and water quality:** a TCU student researcher studied riverbank erosion on the North Canadian River, on Muscogee lands in Oklahoma. The project framed the river's health as a relative. Methods included decades of historical aerial imagery to track river movement and land loss, soil maps and wetland records, field and laboratory water testing, and a literature review on irrigation and riparian systems. The student proposed nature-based bank stabilization with native willows and plantings and priced the work, and told the river's story as one of change: pollution, a changing climate, and a channel that no longer matches the old data.
> - **Soil, water, and community gardens:** a TCU student researcher studied soil and water quality affecting community gardens in a small Oklahoma town, including high mineral levels, the history of aging water infrastructure, and how storms, wind, heat, and drought affect crops. The plan paired soil testing through an extension office and home water testing with trials of new planting techniques informed by Indigenous and scientific knowledge, and community workshops to share results.
> - **Shared tools:** both students compared local observations with CoCoRaHS, the Oklahoma Mesonet, and the U.S. Drought Monitor, used regional climate projections, and filled in the What's Known, What's Under Threat, Projected Impacts, Methods, and Mitigation framing worksheet used in this course.
>
> **Lessons they offered the next generation:** "Explain things as you would to your children or an Elder." "If you're willing to fail at something, you'll figure it out." "We were a community that educated each other." "Better outcome with hands-on learning than just sitting looking at a book."
