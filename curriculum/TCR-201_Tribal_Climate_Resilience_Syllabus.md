## How to Use This Document

A complete syllabus and ten weekly lesson plans for a 201-level course on Tribal climate resilience as action, written first for Tribal Colleges and Universities (TCUs) and usable at four-year institutions and as professional development for Tribal staff.

- **Four modules:** (1) Atmospheric Drivers of Land and Sea Impacts, Weeks 1 and 2; (2) Downscaling: From the Globe to the Reservation, Weeks 3 and 4; (3) Climate Action: Local Impacts, Readiness, and Resources, Weeks 5 to 7; (4) Planning and Collaboration, Weeks 8 to 10.
- **Sequence:** TCR 201 follows "Climate Change in Indian Country" (CCIC 200), built on NESC 215 at Northwest Indian College (NWIC), which ends with an adaptation plan outline. TCR 201 carries a plan into action: who decides, who pays, who else must be at the table, and what the work looks like on a Tuesday morning in a two-person department.
- **Format:** A 10-week quarter of 150-minute blocks, each splitting into two 75-minute sessions at the marked break, with a 15 to 16 week semester crosswalk.
- **Reconstruction labs:** Students rebuild National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) and National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) visualizations from public data in a spreadsheet.
- **Worked example:** Module 2 uses public projections for the Yakama Nation Reservation, with a stub for any Nation. Public data about a Nation's lands is not the Nation's voice (see Week 4).

## Course Description

Climate impacts are already here. This course is about what Tribal Nations do about them, and how. It starts in the atmosphere, because a Nation deciding what to protect needs to know what drives the change. Students watch NASA visualizations of carbon dioxide and methane, rebuild them from public data, and follow the heat to land and sea. Then they bring the signal to the ground through historic records, Pacific Northwest projections under two Shared Socioeconomic Pathway scenarios (SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0), and Tribal-scale projections for the Yakama Nation Reservation, with a projection card any Nation can use.

The second half is action. Students assess heat, drought, fire, and the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) with the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module; trace those hazards into infrastructure, emergency response, and a Nation's economy; and build a funding strategy by hand before checking it against the TCR Policy Scanner. They review a published Tribal plan, track policy, and read the land an action touches, with Plan Assessor, Policy Sentinel, and the Land Use Analyzer as supporting tools. Finally, they practice coordination and negotiation among jurisdictions and rights holders through Columbia River Basin cases, from the withdrawn Resilient Columbia Basin Agreement to clean energy on sacred ground at Pushpum. Each student ends by presenting an Implementation Package to a mock Tribal Council or invited decision-makers.

Sovereignty is the organizing principle. Climate work is sovereignty work. No one can carry all of this work alone.

## Where This Course Sits in the Pathway

TCR 201 is the postsecondary action course in a pathway that runs from community and educator sessions ("Understanding a Climate of Change"), through Grades 6 to 12 ("Changing Currents Classroom Edition"), to CCIC 200 and this course. All levels share one spine:

1. **Begin with experience, not abstraction:** a visualization, a data file, a plan, a docket, a flood.
2. **Plural ways of knowing:** Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) and Indigenous Knowledges stand alongside Western science, on the Nation's terms.
3. **The Elder-and-child test:** the surest sign you understand something is being able to pass it on.
4. **Data literacy as sovereignty:** what data is useful, how it leads to action, and who gets to see it.
5. **From learning to leading:** CCIC 200 ends with a plan outline; TCR 201 ends with a decision in front of decision-makers.
6. **Relationships matter:** it is going to take all of us.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Explain** the sources and measurement of carbon dioxide and methane, and rebuild at least one NASA or NOAA visualization from public data.
2. **Interpret** how added heat reaches land and sea (anomalies and baselines, land versus ocean, sea ice, sea level) and connect it to Pacific Northwest waters, coasts, and First Foods.
3. **Explain** downscaling, bias correction, ensembles, and Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs); read projections under SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0; and explain why even downscaled data differ for Tribes.
4. **Communicate** one climate signal from global to local in plain language, every figure sourced and dated, the scenario named, and uncertainty stated.
5. **Assess** a Nation's observed and projected heat, drought, fire, and ENSO impacts and its infrastructure, emergency, and economic exposure, and distinguish adaptation, mitigation, and resilience.
6. **Construct** a verified funding strategy (sources, eligibility, match, indirect costs, capacity, risks, braiding) that translates between "climate" and "natural hazard" language without losing the Nation's purpose.
7. **Analyze** a published Tribal plan, the policy that governs an action, and the land it touches, by hand first and then with tools.
8. **Map** the jurisdictions, rights holders, and partners an action touches, and practice consultation, InterTribal resolutions, and negotiation grounded in reserved Treaty rights, the trust responsibility, and self-determination.
9. **Apply** the CARE Principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, and Ethics), OCAP (Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession), and the Tiered Sovereign Data Framework (TSDF) to decide what data can be shared, with whom, and how.
10. **Present** an Implementation Package with a one-page decision brief to a council-style audience in five minutes, designed around turnover, grant cliffs, emergencies, and burnout.

## Instructional Approach

- **Rebuild before you trust:** Watch a visualization, then rebuild it from the numbers. If you can rebuild the picture, you can explain it to an Elder and a child and spot when someone else's picture is wrong.
- **Compared to what:** Every number carries its baseline, date, and source; every projection its scenario and period.
- **Hand first, then tools:** Tools change, get defunded, or go offline, so the method must transfer without them. These tools support; they never replace Tribal decision-making.
- **Verify every term; cut tedium that doesn't matter:** Programs, rules, courts, and data feeds changed repeatedly in 2025 and 2026. Verify from primary sources and record the date. Put effort into work that protects the Nation (accurate reporting, consultation records, data governance).
- **Reflection without ratings:** Three open questions each week, read for content and completion, not scored.

## Signature Assessments and Rubrics

### Signature Assessment 1: Climate Signal Brief (Modules 1 and 2)

**What it is:** A two-page brief tracing one climate signal (days at or above 100 °F, first snow, frozen precipitation, or sea level for a coastal Nation) from the globe to the ground for one Nation or case. It must pass the Elder-and-child test.

**Required elements:**

- **The signal:** Why it matters, using the Nation's priorities only as publicly described or as a knowledge holder has approved.
- **Global:** One NASA global visualization or dataset, with its date.
- **Regional:** One Pacific Northwest downscaled projection, with scenario, period, and baseline.
- **Tribal scale:** One Tribal-scale projection (Yakama or the student's projection card) and what its boundary leaves out.
- **Local observation:** One observed record (weather station, stream gauge, snow telemetry, the Community Collaborative Rain, Hail and Snow Network (CoCoRaHS), gridMET, or Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM) data) or a community observation shared with permission and credited as the person chooses.
- **Uncertainty:** What is well known, what differs across models and scenarios, and what the data cannot say.
- **Figures:** At least one student-made lab figure; every figure sourced and dated.

| Criterion | Exemplary (4) | Proficient (3) | Developing (2) | Emerging (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Signal chain | Global, regional, Tribal, and local pieces form one story | Connected with a gap | Four separate facts | A scale missing |
| Accuracy and sourcing | Every figure accurate, sourced, dated; student figure honest and labeled | Minor gaps | Several unsourced or undated | Errors or invented figures |
| Scenario, baseline, uncertainty | Named every time; uncertainty stated plainly | Named most of the time | Inconsistent | Projection presented as prediction |
| Elder-and-child readability | Plain, warm, every term clear | One or two unexplained terms | Technical in places | Jargon-heavy |
| Sovereignty and permission | Permission for community knowledge; boundary limits named | Mostly careful | Speaks for the Nation in places | Knowledge used without permission, or boundaries presented as jurisdiction |

### Signature Assessment 2: Local Impacts and Readiness Profile (Module 3, Weeks 5 and 6)

**What it is:** A three to four page profile setting what is observed now beside what is projected, then asking whether infrastructure, emergency systems, and the economy are ready.

**Required elements:**

- **Evidence view:** A Dynamic Drought Module view saved as a shareable URL, the hazard cluster and horizon chosen and why, a screenshot, what the impact briefing says, and a cross-check with one other source (the U.S. Drought Monitor, CoCoRaHS, or a National Weather Service (NWS) product).
- **Projection:** The relevant Module 2 projection with scenario and period, and one paragraph on what it could mean if this season's conditions became common.
- **Exposure table (Template H):** Infrastructure, emergency, and economic exposure for the top hazards, with sources, unknowns, the responsible department, and current readiness.
- **Gaps and a candidate action:** The three most important gaps, stated respectfully, and one candidate action labeled by which job it does (adaptation, mitigation, or resilience).

| Criterion | Exemplary (4) | Proficient (3) | Developing (2) | Emerging (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Evidence view | Purposeful view; briefing read accurately; cross-checked | Cross-check thin | Screenshot without interpretation | Missing |
| Observed versus projected | Distinct and clearly connected | Mostly distinct | Blurred | Confused |
| Exposure table | Complete, sourced, unknowns named, responsibility assigned; public information only | Mostly complete | Thin or unsourced | Missing |
| Economic realism | Direct costs, lost revenue, and shifted costs identified | Some identified | Mentioned only | Ignored |
| Gaps and action | Three reasoned gaps; action tied to evidence | Present | Loosely linked | Missing |

### Signature Assessment 3: Funding Strategy Matrix (Week 7)

**What it is:** Tables and a two-page narrative showing how to pay for one action over its full life, not just its first year.

**Required elements:**

- **Hazard-to-program links:** A hand-built table linking the Nation's top three hazards (from the Readiness Profile) to programs. Where the TCR Policy Scanner is available, compare its output: where it agrees, where it differs, and which you trust for what. Without the Scanner, the hand-built table stands alone.
- **Sources:** At least five across at least three categories (federal, state, philanthropic, Tribal own-source, partner or in-kind), each with eligibility, status verified this term (date and URL), award range, match, indirect cost treatment, window, reporting burden, and fit with the Nation's priorities.
- **Braiding or stacking plan:** How sources combine without double-counting, and without federal funds matching other federal funds unless a statute allows it.
- **Capacity line:** Who writes, manages, and reports. If the answer is "one person who already has three jobs," say so and address it.
- **Risks and timeline:** A fallback if the lead source is cut, paused, or reframed, and a 12 to 36 month calendar from application to closeout.

| Criterion | Exemplary (4) | Proficient (3) | Developing (2) | Emerging (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hazard-to-program links | Sound links; tool comparison weighs currency and fit | Comparison thin | Links assumed | Missing |
| Verified landscape and eligibility | Every source verified with date and link; eligibility checked line by line | Most verified | Some outdated or assumed | Expired or ineligible programs |
| Match, indirect, and compliance | Allowable match; indirect handled; 2 CFR 200 basics applied | Addressed | Mentioned only | Ignored |
| Braiding and sequencing | Allowable braid or stack, no double-counting | Reasonable | Listed, not combined | Single source |
| Capacity and risk | Capacity gap addressed; fallback for each major risk | Both addressed | One addressed | Neither |

### Signature Assessment 4: Plan, Policy, and Land Review (Week 8)

**What it is:** Three linked parts answering what every funder and council asks before an action moves: does it fit the Nation's plans, what policy governs it, and whose land does it touch?

- **Part A: Plan review.** Review one published Tribal climate adaptation plan (CAP) or hazard mitigation plan with the course rubric (Template C): an assessment, not a grade, centered on the Nation's stated goals. Include a short summary, the rubric with page citations, three gaps stated respectfully, three actions that could start within 12 months with a likely lead, and an alignment note against one federal program (for example, Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) Tribal mitigation plan requirements), noting that a departure may be the Nation's choice.
- **Part B: Policy watch list.** At least eight items (Template D) across federal rules, funding notices, state bills, consultation invitations, InterTribal resolutions, litigation, and regional agreements, each with a recommended response (monitor, brief council, comment, request consultation, coordinate with ATNI, the National Congress of American Indians (NCAI), or the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC), or no action).
- **Part C: Land-use and jurisdiction note (one to two pages)** for one action: who owns and governs the land (trust, fee, federal, state, private); which land use rules apply; what a county assessor record can and cannot tell you; likely permits `[verify with the Nation's staff or counsel]`; and who must be consulted. Describe ceded lands and usual and accustomed areas only as the Nation does.
- **Tool comparison (where available):** One paragraph comparing hand work with Plan Assessor, Policy Sentinel, or the Land Use Analyzer.

| Criterion | Exemplary (4) | Proficient (3) | Developing (2) | Emerging (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plan accuracy and stance | Every finding cited; read on the Nation's terms | Most cited; mostly respectful | Few citations; grades against outside norms | Unsupported or dismissive |
| Gaps and actions | Three significant gaps; three concrete actions with leads | Some thin | Vague | Missing |
| Watch list | Eight or more real items; accurate dates; clear triage | Minor gaps | Fewer items or inaccurate | Invented items |
| Land and jurisdiction | Ownership, governance, and record limits accurate; boundaries handled with care | Mostly accurate | General | Missing, or boundaries presented as jurisdiction |
| Legal and relational grounding | Treaty rights, trust responsibility, and consultation cited accurately | Mostly accurate | General references | Missing or wrong |

### Signature Assessment 5: Capstone Implementation Package (Weeks 9 and 10)

**What it is:** A complete package for one action from the student's own Nation's plan (with permission, and only public information unless the Nation has approved otherwise), or from a Columbia River case or another public Tribal plan.

**Required sections (10 to 14 pages plus appendices):**

1. **Action and evidence:** What, where, why, what it protects, and the evidence from the revised Signal Brief and Readiness Profile.
2. **Lead department and partners:** Who owns it inside the Nation and why; each partner's role.
3. **Coordination map (Template G):** Every jurisdiction, rights holder, and partner the action touches; who decides what; where consultation or negotiation is needed; what is not on the table.
4. **Decision pathway and one-page decision brief (Template A):** Who decides, and when (council calendar, budget cycle, grant window, seasonal round).
5. **Funding, policy, and land:** The revised Matrix and Review, including consultation needed.
6. **Data and sovereignty plan:** Data used and produced, TSDF tier, owner, access, and agreements.
7. **Monitoring and evaluation:** Indicators, including one of the Nation's own measures of health, and how results return to council and community.
8. **Real-world risks and continuity:** Turnover, grant cliffs, emergencies, and burnout, with mitigations, plus the Week 6 "if I leave tomorrow" page.
9. **Presentation:** Five minutes, council-style, with up to five minutes of questions.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Action and evidence | Tied to the plan, the Nation's priorities, and sourced evidence | Minor gaps | Loosely tied | Not tied |
| Coordination map | Every party mapped; authority and negotiation points accurate | Mostly complete | Generic | Missing |
| Decision pathway and brief | Accurate pathway; brief council-ready and Elder-and-child readable | Mostly accurate | Generic | Missing |

## Module 1: Atmospheric Drivers of Land and Sea Impacts (Weeks 1 and 2)

**Module question:** What is changing in the air and the sea, and how do we know? Students watch NASA Scientific Visualization Studio (SVS) visualizations, then rebuild them from public data.

### Week 1: Resilience as Sovereignty, and the Drivers: Carbon Dioxide and Methane

**Key lesson:** Climate resilience is the Nation exercising its sovereignty: deciding what matters, what to protect, and how to carry its relationships and responsibilities through change. That starts with knowing what drives the change. Carbon dioxide (CO2) and methane are rising because of human activity, and anyone can read the record of how much is in the air.

**Key takeaway:** We are sovereign, and we decide what matters. The record of what is in the air is public, and we can read it ourselves.

**Objectives:** Students will be able to:

- Explain resilience as sovereignty, grounded in reserved Treaty rights (U.S. v. Winans, 1905), the federal trust responsibility, and self-determination.
- Name the three jobs of climate work and identify which one acts on the drivers.
- Describe the main sources of CO2 and methane and how their concentrations are measured.
- Rebuild the Keeling Curve and explain its seasonal cycle and trend.

**Readings and viewings:**

- SVS 14631, "Model Behavior: Visualizing Global CO2" (07/22/2024), narrated with captions: https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/14631
- SVS 5447, "Atmospheric Carbon Dioxide Concentrations" (01/02/2025; updated 08/12/2025): https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5447
- SVS 5360, "Global Methane Emissions" (08/21/2024): https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5360

**Mini-lecture: resilience as sovereignty.** In Winans, the U.S. Supreme Court described the Yakama Treaty of 1855 as "not a grant of rights to the Indians, but a grant of right from them, a reservation of those not granted." Climate action by a Nation exercises rights it never gave up. Three jobs organize the course: **mitigation** reduces the change itself (this week's drivers); **adaptation** reduces harm from change already underway; **resilience** is the Nation's ability to absorb, recover, and keep its relationships through both. Each needs its own tools: policy, groundwork, preparation, data, knowledge, funding, and sometimes emergency.

**Watch sheet: SVS 14631.** NASA's Goddard Earth Observing System (GEOS) model shows CO2 in parts per million (ppm) from January to March 2020, at a resolution "more than 100 times greater than your typical weather model," built from ground observations and data from the Terra and Suomi-NPP satellites. Scientists: Lesley Ott (NASA Goddard Space Flight Center) and Brad Weir. Ask: Where do plumes start, how do they mix, and where does the air over the Pacific Northwest come from? TCUs with a planetarium can use the fulldome version, SVS 5333, "DYAMOND Global Carbon Dioxide for Fulldome" (10/07/2024): https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5333

**Mini-lecture: the drivers.**

- **What the gases do:** They let sunlight in and slow the heat going out. Human influence on warming is unequivocal (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), Sixth Assessment Report (AR6)).
- **Carbon dioxide:** Mostly from burning coal, oil, and gas and from clearing land. Plants, soils, and the ocean take some back; the ocean's share makes seawater more acidic (Week 2).
- **Methane:** From landfills, oil and gas, agriculture, and wetlands. It traps more heat per molecule than CO2 and leaves the air sooner, so cutting it is a fast lever on near-term warming. SVS 5360 spins a globe of plumes detected by NASA's Earth Surface Mineral Dust Source Investigation (EMIT) on the International Space Station (data via the EMIT open data portal, VISIONS, at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory). Point sources are visible from space, so they can be found and fixed.
- **How we know:** Monthly measurement at Mauna Loa since 1958 by NOAA and Scripps (the Keeling Curve), and air trapped in Antarctic ice. SVS 5447 shows the seasonal cycle (peak in May, minimum in September) and the upward trend, then expands to 800,000 years using the European Project for Ice Coring in Antarctica (EPICA) Dome C core.

**Lab 1: Rebuild the Keeling Curve.**

- **Data:** NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory (GML) Mauna Loa monthly (`co2_mm_mlo.csv`) and annual (`co2_annmean_mlo.csv`) means: https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends/data.html
- **Steps:**
  1. Open the monthly file; read the header comments, then skip them.
  2. Plot monthly average against decimal date; add the annual means as a second line.
  3. Zoom to three recent years and mark each May peak and September low.
  4. From the annual file, state the change from the first to the latest full year in one sentence, with source and date accessed.
  5. Set your chart beside SVS 5447. The ice core data are in the NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) paleoclimate archive `[verify dataset link]`.
  6. Extension: plot NOAA global monthly methane (https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends_ch4/; from 1983, in parts per billion) and compare shapes.
- **What to look for:** The sawtooth is the Northern Hemisphere breathing: plants take in CO2 as they grow and release it in fall and winter. The trend underneath does not pause. Elder-and-child check: explain both in two sentences, out loud.

### Week 2: Heat Reaches Land and Sea

**Key lesson:** The heat those gases trap goes somewhere. The ocean has absorbed more than 90 percent of the excess heat (IPCC), and land warms faster because it holds less heat. Warming shows up as a shifted, wider spread of temperatures, shrinking sea ice, and rising seas.

**Key takeaway:** Always ask "compared to what?" A number without a baseline, a date, and a source is not ready for a council.

**Objectives:** Students will be able to:

- Define an anomaly and show how a baseline changes the numbers but not the shape of the record.
- Explain why land warms faster than the ocean and what a wider distribution means for extremes.
- Read sea ice and sea level records, name their sources, and connect them to Pacific Northwest waters and coasts.

**Readings and viewings:**

- SVS 5603, "Global Temperature Anomalies from 1880 to 2025" (01/14/2026): https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5603
- SVS 5607, "Record Temperature Years: 2025, 2024, and 2023" (02/04/2026): https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5607
- SVS 5613, "Shifting Distribution of Land Temperature Anomalies, 1964-2025" (02/04/2026): https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5613
- SVS 5677, "Arctic Sea Ice Minimum 2026" (09/23/2026): https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5677

## Module 2: Downscaling: From the Globe to the Reservation (Weeks 3 and 4)

### Week 3: Historic Records and Pacific Northwest Projections

**Key lesson:** Global models describe the planet in coarse boxes. Downscaling translates them to regional scales, using historic observations to correct and sharpen them. A projection is not a prediction; it asks: if the world follows this pathway, what range of climates is likely here?

**Mini-lecture: from models to scenarios, and how to read them.**

- **Scenarios:** SSPs pair a story of how the world develops with a level of greenhouse gas forcing by 2100. IPCC AR6 best-estimate warming for 2081 to 2100, compared with 1850 to 1900: SSP1-2.6 about 1.8 °C (3.2 °F); SSP2-4.5 about 2.7 °C (4.9 °F); SSP3-7.0 about 3.6 °C (6.5 °F); SSP5-8.5 about 4.4 °C (7.9 °F). SSP2-4.5 is a middle-of-the-road pathway; SSP3-7.0 is a high-emissions, fragmented-world pathway. Together they show what is at stake.
- **The bridge:** SVS 5343 shows global maximum monthly temperature from 1950 to 2100 under four SSPs from the NASA Earth Exchange Global Daily Downscaled Projections (NEX-GDDP-CMIP6), a bias-corrected, downscaled version of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) models that supports IPCC AR6. The same dataset underlies the Week 4 Tribal projections.
- **Downscaling:** Statistical downscaling uses relationships between observed local climate and model output; it is fast and runs many models (NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 and Localized Constructed Analogs version 2 (LOCA2) are statistical). Dynamical downscaling runs a regional climate model inside a global one; it is detailed, costly, and covers fewer models. Bias correction adjusts model output so its historic period matches observations.
- **Ensembles and uncertainty:** Report the range and middle across models, never a favorite. Uncertainty comes from models, human choices (scenario), and natural swings.
- **Grid size and baselines:** NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 cells are 0.25 degree, roughly 25 kilometers; gridMET (Climatology Lab) is about 4 kilometers, daily, from 1979; PRISM (Oregon State University) offers 4 kilometer and 800 meter grids, monthly back to 1895. The CIG tool reports change relative to its own historical baseline (1950 to 1999 `[verify]`), a third baseline. Write it down every time.

**Mini-lecture: why even downscaled data differ for Tribes.**

1. **Elevation gets averaged away.** The Yakama Reservation runs from the Yakima Valley floor to the slopes of Mount Adams; one average hides both the valley's heat and the mountain's snow.
2. **Grid cells are coarse.** At 0.25 degree (roughly 25 kilometers), a small reservation or scattered trust parcels may fall in one or two cells.
3. **Boundaries are someone else's.** Census or Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) polygons are not ceded lands or usual and accustomed areas; a Nation's rights and relatives reach past the reservation line.

### Week 6: Infrastructure, Emergency Preparedness, and Economic Impacts

**Key lesson:** Emergencies do not wait for plans, and plans that ignore emergencies do not survive them. Hazards land on water, power, roads, homes, fisheries, budgets, and people. Small staffs, turnover, grant cliffs, consultation fatigue, and burnout are design constraints, not side issues.

**Mini-lecture: emergency and long-term, side by side.**

- **Hazard mitigation plans:** A FEMA-approved Tribal mitigation plan (44 CFR 201.7) is required for many FEMA Hazard Mitigation Assistance funds and is updated every five years. Many Nations have a hazard mitigation plan owned by emergency management and a CAP owned by natural resources; aligning them is one of the most practical things a Nation can do.
- **Declarations and the EOC:** Since the Sandy Recovery Improvement Act of 2013 amended the Stafford Act, federally recognized Tribes may request a declaration directly from the President or be included in a state's request. When an event hits, the EOC runs on ICS; climate staff are pulled in and planning pauses.
- **Infrastructure exposure:** Water and wastewater, power and backup power, roads (and the only road out), housing, cooling and clean-air spaces, and communications.
- **Economic exposure:** Direct costs (overtime, repairs, fuel); lost revenue (fishery and enterprise closures, lost work hours); health costs; and shifted costs, when capped indirect rates or match land on the general fund. A cost log kept during an event is evidence for the next application.
- **Grant cliffs and crisis:** Most climate positions in Indian Country have been grant-funded; when the grant ends, the knowledge walks out the door. The Robinson draft reports that the Nez Perce Tribe's climate program began after the 2015 drought and fish kill: an emergency can open a door if someone is ready.

### Week 7: Grants and Funding

**Key lesson:** Money is not the only bottleneck. Capacity is. A grant a Nation cannot staff, match, manage, or report on can cost more than it brings. Start with the Nation's hazards, priorities, and capacity, not whatever funding notice is open.

**Key takeaway:** Verify everything, every term. Programs are cut, paused, renamed, restarted by courts, and reframed. The Nation's purpose stays the same.

**Objectives:** Students will be able to:

- Link hazards to program eligibility by hand, then compare with TCR Policy Scanner output, verifying status from primary sources.

**Mini-lecture: the mechanics.**

- **Registration:** An active System for Award Management (SAM.gov) registration. Registrations expire, and applications fail here.
- **Match:** Under 2 CFR 200.306, federal funds generally cannot match another federal award unless a statute allows it; some Tribal self-determination funding may qualify (verify with the grants office or counsel).
- **Indirect costs:** Tribes negotiate rates with the Interior Business Center or use the de minimis rate under 2 CFR 200.414 (15 percent after the 2024 revision; verify). Programs that cap indirect costs shift them onto the general fund.
- **Braiding and stacking:** Braiding tracks sources separately on one project; blending pools them (rarely allowed with federal funds); stacking layers them over phases. Never charge one cost twice.
- **Reporting and cliffs:** Reports, closeout, and a single audit above a federal expenditure threshold ($1,000,000 after the 2024 revision; verify). Plan the grant cliff on day one.

Tool: Plan Assessor reads Tribal CAPs, Climate Pollution Reduction Grant (CPRG) plans, and hazard mitigation plans. Its rubric-based assessment, not a grade, shows what is present, what is missing, and where the Nation's priorities align or diverge from federal requirements, so a Nation can see its own plan without outside consultants. **Station 3: Reading the land.** By hand, for your action's site: who owns the land (trust, allotted trust, fee, federal, state, private); who governs its use (Tribal codes, county zoning, federal land plans); what a county assessor record shows (parcel, owner of record, land use code) and cannot (Treaty rights, usual and accustomed areas, cultural significance, water rights, sometimes trust status; ask the Nation's realty office and the BIA). ### Week 9: Coordination, Rights Holders, and Negotiation

**Key lesson:** Rivers, roads, power lines, and fish cross every boundary, so climate action does too. Nations move decisions through many doors: consultation, comments, testimony, InterTribal resolutions, co-management, the media, and, when the relationship fails, the courts. Coordination and negotiation are the work, not an obstacle to it.

**Key takeaway:** Consultation is a process owed to a sovereign, not a meeting granted to a guest. Keep your own record. Know what can be traded and what cannot.

**Objectives:** Students will be able to:

- Distinguish consultation (Executive Order 13175), collaboration, and consent, including free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) under the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), Articles 19 and 32.
- Describe how an InterTribal resolution moves to a vote.
- Map who decides what, negotiate from interests, and keep a consultation record.
- Present a contested case fairly.

**Readings:**

- Executive Order 13175, "Consultation and Coordination With Indian Tribal Governments" (November 6, 2000), via the Federal Register.
- CRITFC on the Resilient Columbia Basin Agreement withdrawal: https://critfc.org/2025/06/12/21153/ ; Oregon Public Broadcasting (OPB), "U.S. pauses Columbia River water-sharing agreement with Canada" (March 2025): https://www.opb.org/article/2025/03/14/us-pauses-columbia-river-water-sharing-agreement-with-canada/
- Washington Department of Ecology, Goldendale Energy Storage Project environmental review, summary (archived): https://web.archive.org/web/20221221210457/https:/apps.ecology.wa.gov/publications/documents/2206015.pdf

**Mini-lecture: the doors.**

- **Consultation:** Requested in writing from the Nation's leadership to the agency head (Template E), with the Nation keeping its own record. Many Nations get more invitations than they can staff; triage is legitimate.
- **Comments and testimony:** Proposed rules appear in the Federal Register with a regulations.gov docket. Effective comments cite the provision, show the effect on the Nation, and propose alternative language.
- **InterTribal resolutions:** A member Tribe sponsors a resolution to ATNI; a standing committee amends and recommends it; the General Assembly votes; it may go on to NCAI. `[PLACEHOLDER: verify current ATNI and NCAI procedures and deadlines]`
- **Co-management:** CRITFC coordinates for CTUIR, the Confederated Tribes of the Warm Springs Reservation of Oregon, the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation, and the Nez Perce Tribe, which co-manage Columbia River fisheries under U.S. v. Oregon (Belloni, 1969). U.S. v. Washington (Boldt, 1974) affirmed Treaty Tribes' right to up to 50 percent of the harvestable fish.
- **Courts and media:** In 2018 the U.S. Supreme Court let stand, by an equally divided vote, a ruling that Washington had to fix culverts blocking salmon under the Treaty right to take fish. Litigation is the backstop, not the plan. A story told by the right person at the right time can move what data did not.

**Structured deliberation: Pushpum.** Not a debate to win. Clean energy is good; building it on sacred ground is not acceptable to the Nations whose sacred ground it is; doing it without Tribal consultation is a failure of the relationship and, where consultation is required, of the law. All three can be true at once. Four groups present: (1) the Yakama Nation and others opposing the Goldendale pumped storage project, including ATNI and NCAI resolutions (as reported in the Robinson draft); (2) the developer and supporters, who argue long-duration storage helps integrate wind and solar; (3) regulators, including Ecology's finding of "significant and unavoidable adverse impacts" to Yakama historic sites and culturally significant plants; (4) the law: the Treaty of 1855, National Historic Preservation Act Section 106, and Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) licensing. Then ask what meaningful consultation and FPIC would have required, and what siting principles a Nation could adopt first. Verify current status and any FERC hearing statements the Robinson draft reports.

**Case: Columbia River agreements and dam removal.** The Resilient Columbia Basin Agreement, which had paused litigation over dam operations and salmon (verify details), was withdrawn by the federal government on June 12, 2025, by executive action, without Tribal consultation. Shannon Wheeler (Nez Perce): "The truth is that extinction of salmon populations is happening now." The Columbia River Treaty reached an Agreement in Principle on modernization on July 11, 2024; the U.S. paused negotiations in March 2025 amid trade tensions (verify status). Tribes and First Nations were excluded from the 1964 Treaty; U.S. Tribes have joined the review since 2012. Dam removal is the other end of the fight: the Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe led the push that removed the Elwha (1913) and Glines Canyon (1927) dams between 2011 and 2014, the largest dam removal in U.S. history at the time; the Robinson draft reports the Nez Perce Tribe's goal of removing the four lower Snake River dams, which others oppose over hydropower, navigation, irrigation, and cost. Celilo Falls was flooded by The Dalles Dam on March 10, 1957. Ask: what does a Nation do when a federal commitment disappears overnight?

**Activity: Negotiation simulation.** A fictional composite. A Nation proposes reconnecting a floodplain and restoring a cold-water refuge on a salmon creek that crosses trust land, irrigation district land, a county culvert, and a state highway, upstream of Treaty fishing places. The federal grant requires match and permits. The Nation's card lists what cannot be traded: reserved rights, its authority over its own lands, and culturally sensitive information. Prepare (10 minutes); negotiate (20 minutes) while the Nation's team keeps the consultation record; debrief (10 minutes): which interests sat under the positions, who could actually agree, what was never on the table, and what the record caught that memory would lose.

**Resolution sprint.** Groups turn the outcome, or another Columbia Basin issue, into a mock InterTribal resolution (Template F) that cites law accurately and asks for something specific, then swap and act as a mock committee: amend, and recommend or not. Mock resolutions are labeled as classroom exercises, never any Nation's position.

## Working With Tribes Respectfully

- **Consult the local Tribe first.** Before inviting a guest, using a Nation's plan or data in depth, or visiting a site, contact the Nation's education, cultural resources, or natural resources department and follow its protocols.
- **Permission from some is not permission from all.** Approval from one leader, department, or family is not approval from the Nation, and approval from one Nation is not approval from another.
- **Compensate knowledge holders.** Elders, knowledge holders, and Tribal staff are paid for their time and credited as they choose.
- **Do not invent or borrow cultural content.** Teachings, stories, place names, and ceremonies come from the Nation, through its people, on its terms.
- **Use Local Contexts labels and follow CARE and OCAP.** Traditional Knowledge (TK) and Biocultural (BC) Labels (https://localcontexts.org/) signal how knowledge may be used. CARE (Global Indigenous Data Alliance) and OCAP (First Nations Information Governance Centre) guide how Indigenous data is gathered, held, and used.
- **Boundaries are not jurisdiction maps.** Do not present Treaty, ceded, usual and accustomed, Census, or data-product boundaries as definitive jurisdiction without the Nation.
- **Language:** Tribal citizens are rights holders, relatives, community members, and partners, not "stakeholders."
- **Do your homework before you ask.** Tribal staff are busy.

## Data Sovereignty Note

- **Public data first.** Coursework uses public (T0) data unless a Nation has given written permission. Public projections about a Nation's lands are T0; what a Nation adds is tiered by the Nation.
- **Tier every dataset.** Every capstone assigns each dataset a TSDF tier (T0 Rain, T1 Lake, T2 River, T3 Canoe). The Nation decides.
- **No culturally sensitive data in coursework.** T3 information stays out of papers, slides, and shared drives; describe a dependency on it without including it.
- **Data returns to the Nation.** Anything produced with a Nation's permission is offered back to it. The course tools follow the same rules (see Week 8).

## Voices From Practice

From a 2024 AIHEC summer research mentorship with two TCU student researchers in Oklahoma (riverbank erosion on the North Canadian River on Muscogee lands; soil and resilient planting in community gardens), shared anonymously:

## Glossary

- **Adaptation:** Reducing harm from climate change already underway or expected.
- **Anomaly:** The difference between a value and the average over a baseline period.
- **Baseline:** The reference period for an anomaly or change (1850 to 1900, 1951 to 1980, 1950 to 1999). Always name it.
- **Bias correction:** Adjusting model output so its historic period matches observations.
- **Braiding and stacking:** Combining sources on one project while tracking each separately; layering sources over phases.
- **Climate adaptation plan (CAP):** A Nation's plan of values, vulnerabilities, priorities, actions, and monitoring for climate impacts.
- **Co-benefit and maladaptation:** A second good from one action; an action that increases vulnerability elsewhere, later, or for someone else.
- **Co-management:** Government-to-government management of shared resources, as under U.S. v. Oregon.
- **Consultation, collaboration, consent:** An agency seeking and considering a Nation's views before acting, without requiring agreement; shared work toward shared goals; the Nation can say no and the answer holds.
- **Downscaling:** Translating global model output to finer scales, **statistically** (from observed relationships) or **dynamically** (a regional model inside a global one).
- **El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO):** A recurring tropical Pacific pattern with warm El Niño and cool La Niña phases that shifts weather far away, including Pacific Northwest winters.
- **Ensemble:** A set of model runs that shows the range of possible outcomes.
- **Free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC):** The UNDRIP standard (Articles 19 and 32) that Indigenous Peoples' consent be sought freely, before a decision, with full information.
- **Global mean sea level (GMSL):** The worldwide average height of the ocean surface, measured by satellite since 1993.
- **Grant cliff:** When a grant ends and what it funded ends with it.
- **Hazard mitigation plan:** A plan to reduce hazard risk; FEMA approval (44 CFR 201.7) unlocks many mitigation funds and requires updates every five years.
- **HeatRisk:** An NWS rating of heat-related health risk for the next seven days.
- **Indirect cost rate and match:** A project's share of the Nation's administrative costs (2 CFR 200.414); the recipient's share of project costs (2 CFR 200.306).
- **Mitigation:** Reducing emissions or storing carbon. "Hazard mitigation" in emergency management means reducing hazard risk. Know which a program means.
- **North American Drought Monitor:** A tri-national consensus drought map for Canada, the United States, and Mexico, via NOAA NCEI.
- **Reserved rights:** Rights Tribes kept in treaties, not rights granted to them (U.S. v. Winans, 1905).
- **Resilience:** A Nation's capacity to absorb, recover from, and adapt to change while keeping its relationships, responsibilities, and self-determination.
- **Sea surface height anomaly (SSHA):** How far the ocean surface stands above or below its long-term average at a place and time.
- **Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP):** A scenario pairing a story of world development with a forcing level by 2100: SSP2-4.5 (middle of the road), SSP3-7.0 (high emissions, fragmented world). An "if," not a forecast.
- **Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK):** Knowledge of relationships among living beings and their environment, carried across generations by Indigenous Peoples; part of Indigenous Knowledges.
- **Trust responsibility:** Federal legal and moral obligations to Tribal Nations arising from treaties, statutes, and the government-to-government relationship.
- **Usual and accustomed places:** Places where Treaty Tribes reserved the right to fish, including off-reservation.
