## Key lesson

Rivers, roads, power lines, and fish cross every boundary, so climate action does too. Nations move decisions through many doors: consultation, comments, testimony, InterTribal resolutions, co-management, the media, and, when the relationship fails, the courts. Coordination and negotiation are the work, not an obstacle to it.

## Key takeaway

Consultation is a process owed to a sovereign, not a meeting granted to a guest. Keep your own record. Know what can be traded and what cannot.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Distinguish consultation (Executive Order 13175), collaboration, and consent, including free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) under the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), Articles 19 and 32 [verify].
- Describe how an InterTribal resolution moves to a vote, from the organization's own published procedure.
- Map who decides what, find the interests under stated positions, and keep an analyst's record.
- Present a contested case fairly, with each party represented only by its own public words, cited.

## Hands-on activity

**Mini-lecture notes: the doors.**

- **Consultation:** Requested in writing from the Nation's leadership to the agency head (Template E), with the Nation keeping its own record. Many Nations get more invitations than they can staff; triage is legitimate.
- **Comments and testimony:** Proposed rules appear in the Federal Register with a regulations.gov docket. Effective comments cite the provision, show the effect on the Nation, and propose alternative language.
- **InterTribal resolutions:** In the source course's example, a member Tribe sponsors a resolution to the Affiliated Tribes of Northwest Indians (ATNI); a standing committee amends and recommends it; the General Assembly votes; it may go on to the National Congress of American Indians (NCAI) [verify]. Here, learn the procedure of {{region.intertribal}} from its own published pages. `[PLACEHOLDER: verify current procedures and deadlines]`
- **Co-management (shared case):** CRITFC coordinates for the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, the Confederated Tribes of the Warm Springs Reservation of Oregon, the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation, and the Nez Perce Tribe, which co-manage Columbia River fisheries under *U.S. v. Oregon* (Belloni, 1969) [verify]. *U.S. v. Washington* (Boldt, 1974) affirmed Treaty Tribes' right to up to 50 percent of the harvestable fish [verify].
- **Courts and media:** In 2018 the U.S. Supreme Court let stand, by an equally divided vote, a ruling that Washington had to fix culverts blocking salmon under the Treaty right to take fish [verify]. Litigation is the backstop, not the plan. A story told by the right person at the right time can move what data did not.

**Position map: the Goldendale pumped storage case (shared case).** Not a debate to win, and no learner speaks as any party. Four analyst groups each present, in the third person, a fair, cited summary built only from one party's own public words: (1) the Yakama Nation's own public statements, and any ATNI and NCAI resolutions as those organizations published them (the source course reports such resolutions from the Robinson draft [verify]); (2) the developer and supporters, who argue long-duration storage helps integrate wind and solar; (3) regulators, including Ecology's finding of "significant and unavoidable adverse impacts" to Yakama historic sites and culturally significant plants [verify]; (4) the law: the Treaty of 1855, National Historic Preservation Act Section 106, and Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) licensing [verify]. Each quotation names its speaker, document, and date ("The Yakama Nation's statement of [date] says..."), never "we." The site's name in the Yakama language, and anything about the site's cultural meaning, is used only as the Yakama Nation itself publishes it: `[NEEDS TRIBAL CONFIRMATION: Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation]`. Then ask, as analysts: what would meaningful consultation and FPIC have required, and what siting principles could a Nation adopt first? Clean energy can be good, building on a place a Nation holds sacred can be unacceptable to that Nation, and skipping consultation is a failure of the relationship and, where consultation is required, of the law: all three can be true at once. Verify current status, and any FERC hearing statements the Robinson draft reports, this term.

**Case: Columbia River agreements and dam removal (shared case).** All facts here are from the source course and are verify items this term.

- The Resilient Columbia Basin Agreement, which had paused litigation over dam operations and salmon, was withdrawn by the federal government on 06/12/2025 by executive action, without Tribal consultation [verify]. CRITFC published the words of Shannon Wheeler (Nez Perce): "The truth is that extinction of salmon populations is happening now." [verify]
- The Columbia River Treaty reached an Agreement in Principle on modernization on 07/11/2024; the U.S. paused negotiations in 03/2025 [verify status]. Tribes and First Nations were excluded from the 1964 Treaty; U.S. Tribes have joined the review since 2012 [verify].
- The Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe led the push that removed the Elwha (1913) and Glines Canyon (1927) dams between 2011 and 2014, the largest dam removal in U.S. history at the time [verify]. The Robinson draft reports the Nez Perce Tribe's goal of removing the four lower Snake River dams, which others oppose over hydropower, navigation, irrigation, and cost [verify].
- Celilo Falls was flooded by The Dalles Dam on 03/10/1957 [verify].

Ask: what does a Nation do when a federal commitment disappears overnight? Learners answer from what Nations have publicly said and done, cited.

**Interest-mapping lab (TEACHING MODEL).** A fictional composite. A floodplain reconnection and cold-water refuge is proposed on a salmon creek that crosses trust land, irrigation district land, a county culvert, and a state highway, upstream of Treaty fishing places. A federal grant requires match and permits. Learners are analysts on a consultant team hired by the county to prepare for government-to-government consultation. Written cards give the interests, stated position, authority (what the party can agree to today), and constraints of four fictional agencies: county public works, the state transportation agency, the irrigation district, and a federal program officer.

The Nation's seat and the InterTribal fisheries co-manager's seat are not played and not written. Their cards say: "A real Nation states its own position. Not on the table, as Template G lists it: reserved rights, the Nation's authority over its own lands, culturally sensitive information, and anything else the Nation names."

1. **Prepare (10 minutes):** map each agency card's interests under its position, and who can agree today.
2. **Practice (20 minutes, optional roles):** students may practice interest-based negotiation among the four agency roles only, as staff of fictional agencies. Students may decline any role. The analyst team keeps its own meeting record (date, who, what was said, what was asked, commitments, follow-up).
3. **Debrief (10 minutes):** which interests sat under the positions; who could actually agree; what was never on the table; what questions the county must bring to the Nation in consultation, and what it must not ask for; what the record caught that memory would lose.

**Resolution language lab.** Groups draft resolution LANGUAGE, as analysts, for review: they turn the lab outcome or another shared-case issue into Template F, which carries the label "CLASSROOM EXERCISE: NOT AN ADOPTED RESOLUTION." The draft cites law accurately, states sourced facts, and asks for something specific, of someone specific. Groups swap drafts and review them with a checklist (accurate citations, sourced facts, a specific ask, plain words); reviewers suggest edits. No group recommends, votes on, adopts, or sponsors a resolution as a Nation, committee, or organization. Only a Nation or organization, through its own process, does that.

**Hand twin and other ways in:** Everything runs on printed cards and paper. Students may build the coordination web as string or lines on a table map, and may present a position summary aloud with the quotations read from the page, or in writing.

## Check for Understanding

1. In one sentence each, how are consultation, collaboration, and consent different?
   Listen for: consultation seeks and considers a Nation's views without requiring agreement; collaboration is shared work toward shared goals; consent means the Nation can say no and the answer holds.
2. Read one sentence from your position map. How does a listener know whose words they are?
   Listen for: speaker, document, and date named; third person; a quotation, not a paraphrase passed off as the party's voice.
3. Name one thing that was never on the table in the interest-mapping lab, and who decides that.
   Listen for: reserved rights, authority over its own lands, culturally sensitive information; the Nation decides, not the analysts.

## Sovereignty thread

Ring 1: The Nation; Ring 2: InterTribal; Ring 3: International. A Nation acts through its own consultation and its own record, with other Nations through InterTribal resolutions, and in the wider world through standards such as UNDRIP's free, prior, and informed consent. Resilience as sovereignty means each door is the Nation's to open or close.
