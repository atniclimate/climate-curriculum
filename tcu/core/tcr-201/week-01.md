## Key lesson

Climate resilience is the Nation exercising its sovereignty: deciding what matters, what to protect, and how to carry its relationships and responsibilities through change. That starts with knowing what drives the change. Carbon dioxide (CO2) and methane are rising because of human activity, and anyone can read the record of how much is in the air.

## Key takeaway

We are sovereign, and we decide what matters. The record of what is in the air is public, and we can read it ourselves.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Explain resilience as sovereignty, grounded in reserved rights, the federal trust responsibility, and self-determination, using one court case as an example (U.S. v. Winans, 1905, in the shared case).
- Name the three jobs of climate work (mitigation, adaptation, resilience) and identify which one acts on the drivers.
- Describe the main sources of CO2 and methane and how their concentrations are measured.
- Rebuild the Keeling Curve by hand and on a spreadsheet, and explain its seasonal cycle and trend.

## Hands-on activity

**Lab 1: Rebuild the Keeling Curve.** Data view label: SNAPSHOT (the instructor's dated capture of the NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory Mauna Loa monthly and annual means; source, issuer, capture date, and checksum on the view). The NOAA file fills some missing months, and its own header notes months in 2022 to 2023 measured at Maunakea rather than Mauna Loa [verify]; read the header and say so.

1. **By hand first.** On the printed table, plot three recent years of monthly values on grid paper. Mark each yearly high and low. Hand twin: the same table, read aloud by a partner, plotted with sticky dots on a wall grid.
2. **On a spreadsheet.** Open the monthly SNAPSHOT file; read the header comments, then skip them. Plot the monthly average against the decimal date; add the annual means as a second line.
3. Zoom to three recent years and mark each yearly peak and low. The high usually comes in May and the low usually in September; check whether every year in your table follows that pattern.
4. From the annual file, state the change from the first to the latest full year in one sentence, with the source and the capture date.
5. Set your chart beside SVS 5447. The ice core data are in the NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) paleoclimate archive [verify] (dataset link).
6. Extension: plot NOAA global monthly methane (https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends_ch4/; from 1983, in parts per billion), captured as a SNAPSHOT, and compare shapes.

**What to look for:** The sawtooth is the Northern Hemisphere breathing: plants take in CO2 as they grow and release it in fall and winter. The trend underneath does not pause. Elder-and-child check: explain both in two sentences, out loud.

**More than one way in:** draw the curve, say it, or write it. A student who cannot use the spreadsheet completes the lab fully by hand.

**No internet:** everything above runs from the printed table and the case pack copy.

## Instructor notes

- Set the expectation early: no one speaks for a Nation in this room, and no one is asked to. Students analyze and may quote a Nation's own public words, short and cited; no student speaks or acts as a Nation, council, Elder, or knowledge holder.
- **Mini-lecture: resilience as sovereignty.** In the shared case, the U.S. Supreme Court in U.S. v. Winans (1905) described the Yakama Treaty of 1855 as "not a grant of rights to the Indians, but a grant of right from them, a reservation of those not granted." Climate action by a Nation exercises rights it never gave up. The legal footing differs from Nation to Nation (treaties, statutes, executive orders, agreements) [verify]; `[INSTRUCTOR SELECTS: the treaties, statutes, or court cases that bear on the Nations of this region, from primary sources]`. Three jobs organize the course: **mitigation** reduces the change itself (this week's drivers); **adaptation** reduces harm from change already underway; **resilience** is the Nation's ability to absorb, recover, and keep its relationships through both. Each needs its own tools: policy, groundwork, preparation, data, knowledge, funding, and sometimes emergency.
- **Watch sheet, SVS 14631.** NASA's Goddard Earth Observing System (GEOS) model shows CO2 in parts per million (ppm) from January to March 2020, at a resolution "more than 100 times greater than your typical weather model," built from ground observations and data from the Terra and Suomi-NPP satellites (scientists: Lesley Ott, NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, and Brad Weir). Ask: Where do plumes start, how do they mix, and where does the air over {{region.name}} come from? TCUs with a planetarium can use the fulldome version, SVS 5333, "DYAMOND Global Carbon Dioxide for Fulldome" (10/07/2024): https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5333
- **Mini-lecture: the drivers.** The gases let sunlight in and slow the heat going out; human influence on warming is unequivocal (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), Sixth Assessment Report (AR6)). CO2 comes mostly from burning coal, oil, and gas and from clearing land; plants, soils, and the ocean take some back, and the ocean's share makes seawater more acidic (Week 2). Methane comes from landfills, oil and gas, agriculture, and wetlands; it traps more heat per molecule than CO2 and leaves the air sooner, so cutting it is a fast lever on near-term warming. SVS 5360 shows plumes detected by NASA's Earth Surface Mineral Dust Source Investigation (EMIT) on the International Space Station: point sources are visible from space, so they can be found and fixed. We know the amounts from monthly measurement at Mauna Loa since 1958 (with the gaps and fills the file itself notes) by NOAA and Scripps (the Keeling Curve), and from air trapped in Antarctic ice; SVS 5447 expands to 800,000 years using the European Project for Ice Coring in Antarctica (EPICA) Dome C core.
- Data posture: the lab uses a dated SNAPSHOT only; open the NOAA pages yourself outside class. A missing month is unknown, never zero.

Resilience is sovereignty in action: the Nation decides what matters and what to protect (Ring 1: The Nation). The plan inventory starts from the Nation's own published plans, noted and linked rather than summarized, and the drivers are a shared, planet-wide record that Nations also read and answer together (Ring 2: InterTribal; Ring 3: International, where the IPCC's assessment names human influence).
