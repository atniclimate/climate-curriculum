## Key lesson

Action starts with local impacts: what is known, what is observed, and what projections could mean if today's hard seasons become ordinary. Keep observation and projection side by side, and never blur them.

## Key takeaway

The impacts are already here, so we respond quickly and on purpose, without getting lost in fear, the future, and the "black mirror" of media. We must adapt now if we are to be resilient; we must mitigate now if we are to limit the impacts on future generations.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Distinguish current conditions, weeks-ahead and season-ahead outlooks, and long-range projections.
- Read drought, wildfire, heat, and El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) conditions by hand from dated SNAPSHOT views, then use the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module outside class where it covers their region, read its impact briefing, and connect it to their Module 2 projections.
- Sort actions into adaptation, mitigation, and resilience, naming co-benefits and maladaptation.

## Hands-on activity

Every view in class is a SNAPSHOT captured by the instructor, labeled with its source, issuer, capture date, and horizon. The Dynamic Drought Module, the Drought Monitor, and NOAA pages are LIVE and are never run in class. A blank on a map or in a table is unknown, never zero.

**Framing worksheet (hand first, all session).** From American Indian Higher Education Consortium (AIHEC) research mentorship with TCU students: for one issue, fill **What's Known** (data first), **What's Under Threat** (health, land, economy, community, ways of life), **Projected Impacts**, **Methods**, and **Mitigation**, plus a sixth box for 201: **Who Acts?** Keep observed and projected evidence in separate boxes.

**Lab 5A: The brief view and the impact briefing.**

1. **By hand first.** On the printed current-conditions SNAPSHOT for {{institution.state}}, circle the drought category over the Nation or case you follow and write it in the What's Known box with the capture date. Then lay the weeks-ahead and season-ahead printouts beside it. Hand twin: three printouts in a row on the table, read aloud by a partner, with a sticky note on each saying "now," "weeks ahead," or "season ahead."
2. Read the printed impact briefing for your Nation's or case's boundary. The tool composes it from land identity; current, near-term, and long-range drought, with wildfire and extreme heat foregrounded; the seasonal water outlook; ENSO positioning; and public resources routed in stewardship order (the Tribe's own resources first, then federal, then state). Discuss why that order matters.
3. Note what the map is not: the Census Tribal Lands layer and the BIA reservation layer are public reference geography, each a representation, not a statement of jurisdiction. The tool's two deployer slots for Tribal lands and Treaty areas ship as empty placeholders that a deployer may fill with its own authorized data.
4. Shared case: read the `columbia_snake_basin` SNAPSHOT (streamflow and snowpack) beside your own. Cross-check with the Drought Monitor and CoCoRaHS SNAPSHOTs.

**Lab 5B: Hazard cluster rotation.** Each group takes one cluster's SNAPSHOT and reports in three minutes: what is observed now, what the outlook says, what the group's Module 2 projection says, and what that could mean.

- **Drought:** Drought Monitor now; Climate Prediction Center (CPC) outlooks at weeks-ahead and season-ahead.
- **Wildfire:** fire perimeters (wildfire and prescribed fire kept distinct), smoke plumes, the fire-weather outlook, and wildfire hazard potential. Cultural burning is the Nation's knowledge; do not describe it unless the Nation has.
- **Extreme heat:** National Weather Service (NWS) HeatRisk for seven days plus active heat notices, with no season-ahead surface. Ask why. What would your Week 4 heat variable mean for Elders, outdoor workers, and cooling centers?
- **ENSO:** the sea surface temperature anomaly display with the ENSO index snapshot. Connect it to SVS 5663 (Week 2) and to a dated SNAPSHOT of NOAA CPC on what this phase usually means for winter in {{region.name}} [verify].

**Activity: "Three Jobs" card sort.** Cards are printed; each holds one action from public sources.

- Shared case cards, as reported in Steve Robinson's *Climate Change and the Tribes: Columbia River Edition* (draft) [verify]: large wood and beavers returned to Yakima River floodplains (Yakama Nation); a 2022 CTUIR resolution directing non-greenhouse-gas power generation; Nez Perce solar plus storage, electric vehicle chargers, and tree planting; ending water diversions to keep streams cooler (Nez Perce fisheries); and a Warm Springs transmission upgrade reported as funded by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE).
- This region's cards: `[INSTRUCTOR SELECTS: two or three actions from public plans in {{region.name}}, each in the issuing Nation's or agency's own public words, cited]`
- Every site adds: a hazard mitigation plan update, a cooling center, and a drought declaration.

For each card: which jobs, which tools, one co-benefit, one maladaptation risk. Rank what a two-person department could start this year. Each student leaves with a candidate capstone action. Hand twin: cards sorted into three labeled piles on a table (a card may sit across two piles); more than one way to respond: sort, say, or sketch.

## Check for Understanding

1. Sort three printouts (current drought, a season-ahead outlook, and your Week 4 projection) into "observed," "outlook," and "projection." Say why for each.
   Listen for: observed is measured now; an outlook covers weeks or a season ahead; a projection is a long-range "if" under a scenario; they are never blended.
2. Why does the impact briefing list the Tribe's own resources first?
   Listen for: stewardship order; the Nation's own authority and resources come first, then federal, then state.
3. Pick one card. Which job does it do, and what is one way it could go wrong?
   Listen for: a job named (adaptation, mitigation, resilience, or more than one), a co-benefit, and a maladaptation risk (harm elsewhere, later, or to someone else).

## Instructor notes

- **Mini-lecture: known, observed, projected.** Current conditions are measured now; weeks-ahead and season-ahead outlooks are forecasts with stated confidence; long-range projections are scenario-based "if" statements (Week 3). Keep them in separate boxes on every page students write.
- Live data change daily. Capture each cluster the day before class; if a feed is down, say so and show the capture that records it.
- The Dynamic Drought Module's region presets favor the Pacific Northwest, with national, Alaska, and Hawaii options; check that the layers your class needs report data for your region before you plan around them.
- Shared case cards come from a draft secondary source; carry them as "as reported in" and verify each against the issuing Nation's or agency's own public words before teaching. The source course listed numbers for one card; they are not carried here until a source is opened.
- Some students have lived through drought, fire, or heat emergencies. Offer choice in how deeply they engage, and name support resources. `[PLACEHOLDER: campus counseling and community wellbeing resources]`

## Sovereignty thread

The stewardship order (the Tribe's own resources first) and the empty Tribal and Treaty slots that only a Nation fills both say the same thing: the Nation decides what data describes its lands (Ring 1: The Nation). Drought and fire cross every line, so the observed record is shared work among Nations and agencies (Ring 2: InterTribal), and ENSO ties this place to an ocean-wide pattern (Ring 3: International).
