## Key lesson

Emergencies do not wait for plans, and plans that ignore emergencies do not survive them. Hazards land on water, power, roads, homes, fisheries, budgets, and people. Small staffs, turnover, grant cliffs, consultation fatigue, and burnout are design constraints, not side issues.

## Key takeaway

It's going to take all of us. No one can carry all of this work alone. Build the work so it survives any one person leaving, including you.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Explain how a Tribal hazard mitigation plan connects to Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) funding and to a climate adaptation plan (CAP), and how declarations and an Emergency Operations Center (EOC) work under the Incident Command System (ICS) and the National Incident Management System (NIMS) [verify].
- Build an infrastructure, emergency, and economic exposure table (Template H) for the shared case and for this place.
- Describe how a decision moves inside a government, and write a one-page brief for a governing body the student does not speak for.

## Hands-on activity

**Part 1: Exposure table, hand first (Template H).** On paper, each group fills Template H for two hazards from its Readiness Profile. Every row names what is exposed (infrastructure, emergency, or economic), what is known (source and date), what is unknown, the responsible department or partner, and readiness (in place, partial, not found, may be held internally). A blank is "unknown", never zero, and "not found" in a public plan is not "missing": it may be held internally. Shared case first: {{case.shared}}, using the public plan the instructor selected. Then this place, from the Local lesson.

Then the tool: where the class used the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module in Week 5, set the group's hand-built rows beside the instructor's dated SNAPSHOT of the module's impact briefing for the same area. Where do they agree, and what did each miss? The module covers North America, with the richest layers in the United States; check that your region's view carries data before relying on it (where it covers your region). Tools support and never replace Tribal decision-making.

**Part 2: Tabletop exercise (TEACHING MODEL).** A fictional composite for training. It describes no real government's response, and no learner plays a Nation, a council, an Elder, or a knowledge holder.

Setting: a fictional county and its public utility district in late July, on a river in the shared case. Learners are planning analysts and emergency-management staff of the county or the utility district, or a consultant team hired by them. Staff roles: county emergency manager, utility operations lead, county public health lead, county finance officer (keeps the cost log), communications lead, and a state agency contact. `[LOCAL EXPERT: TCU faculty or knowledge holder]` may adjust the roles to fit local emergency management.

- **Inject 1:** A multi-day heat wave. A public notice reports river temperatures above 21 °C at a monitoring site [verify] and dead adult salmon. Elders and others without air conditioning are at risk.
- **Inject 2:** Wildfire smoke arrives. The utility warns of possible public safety power shutoffs. A cooling center needs backup power.
- **Inject 3:** A federal agency notice on a change to river operations arrives with a 30-day comment window. The county's climate coordinator position has been vacant for four months.

The Nation or Nations of the area are governments with their own emergency management. Their seat at this table is not played. Groups write what they would ask of, and offer to, each Nation's emergency management office, and how they would reach it through its own protocols; they do not write the answer. Groups decide actions for the first 72 hours and the first 30 days while the finance officer keeps a cost log.

After-action review: What should change in the county hazard mitigation plan? What should have been ready before July? Which job (adaptation, mitigation, or resilience) did each decision serve?

**Part 3: After-action brief (Template A).** A good idea that does not reach the people who decide, when they decide, in a form they can use, is not yet an action. Cover the pathway inside a government (staff, director, committee, executive, governing body; in some Nations a general council) and its calendars (meetings, budget cycles, grant windows, seasonal rounds). Groups draft a one-page brief asking for one change before next July, addressed to the county commission or utility board, or to a Tribal council that has invited such a briefing. No learner plays the council. The instructor reads questions from a written prompt card, and the group answers as the staff it is:

- What does this do for Treaty rights and the people they protect?
- What does it cost, and who pays?
- What does the community need this week?
- "What does the Elders' committee think?" (A staff group does not answer for Elders. The right answer names who would be asked, by whom, and through what protocol.)

`[LOCAL EXPERT: TCU faculty or knowledge holder]` may add prompt-card questions a local governing body would ask.

**Hand twin and other ways in:** Every part runs on paper with no internet. Students may draw the exposure chain (hazard, to what is exposed, to who is affected) instead of writing the table first, and may answer the prompt card aloud, in writing, or as a sketch of the decision pathway.

## Check for Understanding

1. Name one thing a hazard mitigation plan does that a climate adaptation plan usually does not, and one reason a Nation might want the two aligned.
   Listen for: hazard mitigation plans are tied to FEMA funding and emergency management; adaptation plans carry long-term climate actions and the Nation's priorities; alignment lets one action count in both.
2. In your exposure table, point to one row marked "unknown" or "may be held internally." Why is that not a zero?
   Listen for: missing public data is not proof that nothing is exposed; a Nation may hold the information and choose not to publish it.
3. Your brief went to a governing body. Who answers the question "What does the Elders' committee think?"
   Listen for: only that Nation's Elders or their authorized representatives; staff name who would ask and through what protocol.

## Sovereignty thread

Ring 1: The Nation. A Nation decides how it prepares for and answers an emergency, and since 2013 a federally recognized Tribe may request a federal declaration directly [verify]; county and utility staff plan beside it, not for it. Resilience as sovereignty means building work that survives turnover, grant cliffs, and burnout, so the Nation's own decisions hold.
