## Key lesson

The heat those gases trap goes somewhere. The ocean has absorbed more than 90 percent of the excess heat (IPCC), and land warms faster because it holds less heat. Warming shows up as a shifted, wider spread of temperatures, shrinking sea ice, and rising seas.

## Key takeaway

Always ask "compared to what?" A number without a baseline, a date, and a source is not ready for a council.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Define an anomaly and show how a baseline changes the numbers but not the shape of the record.
- Explain why land warms faster than the ocean and what a wider distribution means for extremes.
- Read sea ice and sea level records, name their sources, and connect them to the waters, coasts, or seasons of their region, with the Pacific Northwest as the shared case.

## Hands-on activity

Every view in these labs is a SNAPSHOT from the case pack, labeled with its source, issuer, capture date, and baseline. A blank year is unknown, never zero.

**Lab 2A: Re-baseline the record.**

1. **By hand first.** On the printed table, plot ten decades (one value per decade, given on the sheet) as bars against the 1951 to 1980 baseline. Then subtract the 1880 to 1900 mean (on the sheet) from each and replot on tracing paper laid over the first. Hand twin: the same bars built from paper strips cut to length.
2. **On a spreadsheet.** Plot the January to December annual anomalies as bars from 1880 (the 1951 to 1980 baseline of SVS 5603). GISTEMP starts in 1880, so subtract the 1880 to 1900 mean as a labeled stand-in for 1850 to 1900 and replot. Compare both with SVS 5607.
3. **What to look for:** The shape stays; every bar's height moves. Which baseline would you use with a council, and how would you say it?

**Lab 2B: Three stations.** Each station has a printed table and a spreadsheet file; a group may finish either.

- **Land versus ocean:** From the Climate at a Glance SNAPSHOT, plot global annual anomalies for land only and ocean only, and record the page's baseline. Rewatch SVS 5613: what does a wider spread mean for your community's hottest days?
- **Sea ice:** SVS 5677 follows Arctic sea ice from the 03/15/2026 maximum to the 09/12/2026 minimum of 1.78 million square miles (4.60 million square kilometers); the yellow line is the 1981 to 2010 average minimum. Data come from the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's SHIZUKU satellite, with the National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC). Rebuild the record from the September extents SNAPSHOT. Do not quote a 2026 rank until NSIDC confirms it. Melting sea ice does not raise sea level; it changes how much sunlight the Arctic reflects.
- **Sea level:** SVS 5620 shows global mean sea level from 1993 to 2025 as a 60-day smoothed water line against a ruler, from satellite altimetry (TOPEX/Poseidon through Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich). Plot the SNAPSHOT with a trend line. Seas rise because warmer water expands and land ice melts. Then scan SVS 5663 (weekly sea surface height, roughly minus 0.5 to plus 0.5 meters) for a tropical Pacific pattern that might be El Niño or La Niña; the instructor brings a dated SNAPSHOT of NOAA's ENSO status [verify] (current page address).

**More than one way in:** each station can be answered by a chart, a sentence said aloud, or a sketch. **No internet:** the SVS videos and every table come from the case pack.

## Check for Understanding

1. Two charts show the same years, one against 1951 to 1980 and one against 1880 to 1900. What changed and what stayed the same? Say it, or point on the two printouts.
   Listen for: every bar's height moved; the shape of the record did not; always name the baseline.
2. Why does land warm faster than the ocean, and what does a wider spread of temperatures mean for the hottest days?
   Listen for: the ocean holds more heat; a wider, shifted spread means more extremes, not only a higher average.
3. Does melting sea ice raise sea level? What does it change?
   Listen for: no, it already floats; it changes how much sunlight the Arctic reflects. Seas rise from warming water expanding and land ice melting.

## Instructor notes

- **Mini-lecture: where the heat goes.** An anomaly is the difference from a baseline average. SVS 5603 maps NASA's GISS Surface Temperature Analysis (GISTEMP v4) against 1951 to 1980, built on NOAA Global Historical Climatology Network (GHCN v4) stations and Extended Reconstructed Sea Surface Temperature (ERSST v5) ocean data. SVS 5607 morphs monthly data into annual bars against 1850 to 1900 across the 146-year NASA record, in English and Spanish. Different baselines, different numbers, same planet. The recent decade is about 1.1 to 1.3 °C (2.0 to 2.4 °F) above 1850 to 1900; the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) put single years at about 1.45 °C (2023) and 1.55 °C (2024) above pre-industrial. A year is not a decade. SVS 5613, the "climate jellyfish," shows the bell curve of land temperature anomalies (against 1951 to 1980) sliding right and spreading out: by 2025 the peak sits about 1.2 °C (2.1 °F) above baseline and the distribution is nearly twice as wide (scientists: Gavin Schmidt, NASA Goddard Institute for Space Studies, and Helga Kleiven, University of Bergen).
- SVS 5677 was published one day before the source course draft; confirm NSIDC's final ranking of the 2026 minimum before quoting a rank.
- Relocation and shoreline plans: discuss from the Nation's own public materials only, as engineering, funding, and law, and also homes, burial places, identity, and who pays. Ask what a Nation decides itself and what it needs from federal partners. Do not speculate about a Nation's choices.
- These visuals can feel heavy. Name climate maturity (syllabus, Course Policies) and let people step out. `[PLACEHOLDER: campus counseling and community wellbeing resources]`
- Data posture: every chart is a dated SNAPSHOT; the NASA, NOAA, and NSIDC sites are LIVE and opened by the instructor outside class only.

## Sovereignty thread

"Compared to what?" is a sovereignty question: a Nation that names its own baselines and asks for the source before it acts is reading the record on its own terms (Ring 1: The Nation). The heat is planet-wide, so the record is shared work across Nations and countries (Ring 3: International).
