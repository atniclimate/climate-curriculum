## Key lesson

Funding requires policy adherence, prior work included and understood, and land use considered. Funders ask whether an action fits the plans, follows the rules, and can be built where proposed. The Nation asks harder questions: does it serve our priorities, protect our rights, and respect our relatives?

## Key takeaway

Do it by hand first so you know what the tool is doing. Then use the tool to eliminate tedium where it doesn't matter, and spend the saved time where it does: with people.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Read a published Tribal plan respectfully against a rubric, set up a policy watch list, and describe who owns and governs the land an action touches.
- Compare hand work with Plan Assessor, Policy Sentinel, and the Land Use Analyzer where each is available and covers the region.
- Sort data into Tiered Sovereign Data Framework (TSDF) tiers and name who decides.

## Hands-on activity

**Mini-lecture notes.** A climate adaptation plan (CAP) is a portfolio of actions (no-regrets, low-regret, staged, and transformative) built around what the Nation protects. Funders check that an action appears in an adopted plan (often a FEMA-approved hazard mitigation plan [verify]), follows program rules, and has land and permits addressed. The Nation checks its own priorities first.

**Station 1: Reading a plan.** By hand: complete three rows of the Plan Review Rubric (Template C) on the shared-case plan summary, citing pages. Mark each row Present, Partial, Not found, or May be held internally. Write findings respectfully ("The summary does not name a lead for Action X; this may be in the full plan," not "The plan fails to..."). An assessment, not a grade, centered on the Nation's stated goals. Then the tool, where available: Plan Assessor (ATNI Climate) is a local tool that, by its own README (read 10/15/2026), extracts text, layout, and tables from PDF plans on the user's own machine, keeps each observation tied to its source page, and builds cited evidence; "Nothing is uploaded." The instructor shows a dated SNAPSHOT of its output on the same plan. Compare what each caught.

**Station 2: Watching policy.** By hand: set up Federal Register alerts, regulations.gov docket tracking, and bill tracking for {{institution.state}} (Template D). Then the tool, where it covers your region: Policy Sentinel (ATNI Climate) is, by its own README (read 10/15/2026), "a source-reference and discovery tool" that "is not legal advice, does not determine rights or legal effect, does not guarantee complete or current coverage." Its public demo searches federal sources and some state sources; its README lists Oregon and Idaho bill lookup as not available, and its intended regional cohort is the Pacific Northwest and nearby states. Outside that cohort, Station 2 runs by hand. Discuss: what would your Nation keep in a private corpus on its own infrastructure, and what would it keep out?

**Station 3: Reading the land.** By hand, for your action's site: who owns the land (trust, allotted trust, fee, federal, state, private); who governs its use (Tribal codes, county zoning, federal land plans); what a county assessor record shows (parcel, owner of record, land use code) and cannot show (Treaty rights, usual and accustomed areas, cultural significance, water rights, sometimes trust status; ask the Nation's realty office and the BIA). Every boundary on a public map is a representation, not jurisdiction. Then the tool, where it covers your region: the Land Use Analyzer (ATNI Climate) covers Whatcom County, Washington only. By its own README (read 10/15/2026), it records 117,990 parcels, excludes personal names, addresses, prices, and assessed values, has no Reservation, BIA, Treaty, usual and accustomed, or water-rights-claim layer in version 1, and its public deployment is paused. For every other site, Station 3 runs by hand. Discuss: why would a tool leave those layers off, and who should decide what goes on them? Lands and usual and accustomed areas are described only as each Nation describes them: `[NEEDS TRIBAL CONFIRMATION: the Nation(s) whose homelands the campus is on]`.

**TSDF data sort.** The Tiered Sovereign Data Framework uses a watershed: T0 Open (Rain: released for public benefit by the Nation's decision), T1 Network (Lake: shared among Indigenous network members through reciprocal protocols), T2 Negotiated (River: shared with outside partners through formal agreements), and T3 Sovereign (Canoe: culturally sensitive, under the community's complete control). Groups tier printed data cards (public streamflow, a water quality result, a First Foods gathering area, recorded Elder interviews, a council resolution, fish counts shared among co-managers, a map of Treaty fishing places), then name who decides. The answer is always the Nation. When unsure, treat it as T3. The cards name kinds of data only; no card holds real protected content.

**Hand twin and other ways in:** All three stations and the sort run on paper. Students may tier cards by placing them on a drawn watershed (rain, lake, river, canoe), and may report a station aloud, in writing, or as a marked-up printout.

## Check for Understanding

1. A plan does not mention one rubric element. Write the finding the respectful way.
   Listen for: "not found in the public plan; may be held internally," with a page cited, and no "the plan fails."
2. Name two things a county assessor record cannot tell you about a parcel.
   Listen for: Treaty rights, usual and accustomed areas, cultural significance, water rights, sometimes trust status; and who to ask (the Nation's realty office, the BIA).
3. Hold up the card "recorded Elder interviews." Which tier, and who decides?
   Listen for: treated as T3 unless the Nation says otherwise; the Nation decides, never the student or the tool.

## Instructor notes

- Public plans leave out what Nations choose not to publish; a missing item may be protected knowledge, which is why the rubric includes "may be held internally." People share data when they control what happens to it.
- Tool coverage differs by region. Check each tool's README before the term; where a tool does not cover your region or is not available, the hand station is the whole lab, not a lesser version.
- Never put a Nation's internal documents, culturally sensitive information, or data above T0 into a public AI tool or a public demo.

## Sovereignty thread

Ring 1: The Nation. A Nation's plan, its codes, and its realty records are its own; reading them on the Nation's terms, and leaving its lands to be described in its own words, is how a learner respects that authority. Data sovereignty is part of resilience: the Nation decides the tier of every piece of its information.
