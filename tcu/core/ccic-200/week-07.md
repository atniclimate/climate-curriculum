## Key lesson

Adaptation, mitigation, and resilience are not the same. They are different jobs with different tools: policy, groundwork, preparation, data, knowledge, funding, and sometimes emergency. Adaptation starts with knowing the hazards already at the door.

## Key takeaway

The impacts are already here, so we respond quickly and on purpose, without getting lost in fear of the future or the "black mirror" of media.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Explain the main climate hazards of {{region.name}} and of the shared case, the Northwest: extreme heat, drought, wildfire and smoke, flooding and atmospheric rivers, snowpack loss, and ocean change (warming, marine heatwaves, acidification, sea level rise).
- Distinguish a hazard from a risk (risk depends on hazard, exposure, and vulnerability).
- Trace a hazard cascade (for example, fire, then rain, then debris flow, then sediment on salmon redds).
- Build a hazard profile for their homelands from dated SNAPSHOT views of public data.

**Hazard tour content notes (Shared case: {{case.shared}}).** These Northwest examples stay the same at every site; the Local lesson sets the hazards of this place beside them.

- **Extreme heat:** the late June 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome set all-time records across the region. Warmer nights matter as much as hot days, because bodies, fish, and buildings do not cool down. The Robinson draft reports Nez Perce climate staff describing Tribal members without air conditioning, and swimming holes made unusable by harmful algal blooms [verify].
- **Drought and snow drought:** in 2015, much of the region's winter precipitation fell as rain instead of snow, leaving low snowpack and low, warm summer flows [verify]. Snow drought is a warming signal.
- **Wildfire and smoke:** heat and drought lengthen fire seasons. Smoke travels far beyond the fire. The 2017 Eagle Creek Fire in the Columbia River Gorge is a regional example.
- **Flooding and atmospheric rivers:** long, narrow plumes of Pacific moisture bring much of the region's heavy rain. Rain on snow and rain on burned slopes raise flood and debris-flow risk.
- **Snowpack loss:** less snow and earlier melt shift water from summer, when fish, farms, and forests need it most, to winter and spring.
- **Ocean change:** warming, marine heatwaves, and acidification (the ocean absorbing CO2) stress shellfish and the salmon food web; sea level rise threatens coastal villages and sites.
- **Cascades:** the Robinson draft describes how, on Nez Perce homelands, fires are followed by floods and landslides that send sediment into streams and smother redds [verify].

## Hands-on activity

**Hazard Profile and Cascade Map.** All views are the instructor's dated SNAPSHOT captures, labeled, printed, and projected. Nothing is run live in class.

1. **Shared case.** Read the captures for the Columbia and Snake Basin: drought, heat, fire perimeters, streamflow, snowpack, and alerts, and the place-based impact briefing the instructor captured. Note each capture's date.
2. **This place.** Read the same kinds of captures for this place, and the public hazard sources in the Local lesson. Note which hazards were active on the capture date and which are seasonal. A hazard with no data in a view is unknown there, not absent.
3. **Cascade map, by hand.** On paper, pick one hazard and draw at least four linked consequences, ending at a relative (a First Food, a person, a place). Mark which links you know from data and which from experience. Students may draw, write, or talk it through with a partner who writes.
4. **Adaptation here.** Name one adaptation already happening in your community for that hazard, from public sources or your own life (a cooling center, fuel reduction, a floodplain project, an alert system).

## Check for Understanding

1. What is the difference between a hazard and a risk? Say it, or draw it.
   Listen for: a hazard is the event (heat, smoke, flood); risk also depends on who and what is exposed and how vulnerable they are, so the same smoke is a bigger risk for an Elder without clean air indoors.
2. Draw or say a cascade of at least three links that starts with one hazard here.
   Listen for: each link causes the next; the chain ends at a relative; the student can say which links come from data and which from experience.
3. Adaptation, mitigation, and resilience are different jobs. Name one tool for any one of them.
   Listen for: adaptation is living with change, mitigation is reducing the cause, resilience is coming through and keeping ways of life; tools include policy, groundwork, preparation, data, knowledge, funding, and sometimes emergency.
