## Key lesson

Carbon moves in a circle through air, ocean, land, and living Beings. Burning fossil carbon, stored underground for millions of years, adds carbon to the air faster than the circle can take it back, and that thickens the greenhouse blanket.

## Key takeaway

We must mitigate now if we are to limit the impacts on future generations. Mitigation is the only job that reduces the cause, and Tribes are already doing it on their own terms.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Describe the global carbon cycle: its reservoirs (atmosphere, ocean, land and living things, fossil carbon) and the flows between them.
- Explain the greenhouse effect and the role of CO2.
- Read the Keeling curve and describe the change in atmospheric CO2 over the last 100 years and beyond.
- Distinguish long-term geologic climate change from recent human-caused change.
- Describe natural climate solutions and Tribal mitigation examples, and when clean energy conflicts with Tribal rights.

**Content notes (Moon's Eye).**

- **Greenhouse effect:** sunlight warms the surface; the surface gives off heat (infrared); CO2, water vapor, methane, and other gases absorb and re-emit part of that heat. Without any greenhouse effect, Earth's average surface temperature would be about minus 18 °C instead of about 15 °C. More CO2 means more heat held.
- **The recent change:** before industrialization, atmospheric CO2 was about 280 parts per million (ppm). CO2 has been measured at Mauna Loa since 1958, with a gap in 2022 to 2023 [verify]. Read the most recent value from the class SNAPSHOT and say its date; recent annual values are above 420 ppm [verify]. Ice cores show CO2 stayed roughly between 180 and 300 ppm over the last 800,000 years.
- **Geologic versus recent change:** over tens of thousands of years, changes in Earth's orbit paced ice ages, with CO2 and temperature rising and falling together over thousands of years. The current rise has happened in about 150 years and tracks fossil fuel burning and land clearing.
- **The ocean:** the ocean has taken up a large share of human CO2 emissions and most of the excess heat. The first slows warming; both change ocean chemistry and life.
- **Natural climate solutions:** Fargione et al. (2018) estimated that 21 natural pathways (forests, grasslands, agricultural lands, and wetlands) could together offset roughly one fifth of U.S. net annual emissions. Many of these pathways overlap with Tribal land stewardship: forest management, cultural burning where Tribes lead it, and wetland and floodplain restoration.
- **Tribal mitigation examples (Shared case: {{case.shared}}; from the Robinson draft; verify details before teaching):** the Nez Perce Tribe's solar and storage, 72 electric vehicle chargers, and about 380,000 trees on 2,137 acres, estimated to reduce about 18,700 metric tons CO2 equivalent by 2030 [verify]; CTUIR solar and battery storage at its food sovereignty center [verify]; Warm Springs transmission and solar work [verify].
- **Clean energy and consent (Shared case):** the proposed Goldendale pumped storage project in Washington sits near a site of great importance to the Yakama Nation. The Robinson draft reports a Washington Department of Ecology finding of "significant and unavoidable adverse impacts" on Yakama cultural resources [verify]. The site's name and meaning are the Nation's to give: [NEEDS TRIBAL CONFIRMATION: Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation]. Frame for discussion: clean energy is needed; clean energy on sacred ground or without meaningful consultation is not climate justice. Quote only the Nation's own public words, short and cited.

## Hands-on activity

**Carbon Cycle Lab.** Works with no network.

1. **Carbon accounts (TEACHING MODEL, hand first).** Label four containers atmosphere, ocean, land and living things, and fossil carbon. Use beans or tokens. Run "years" where carbon moves naturally between the first three (photosynthesis, respiration, ocean exchange). Then add fossil carbon to the atmosphere each year. Students record what happens to the atmosphere's account. Say aloud that the bean counts are a teaching model, not measurements.
2. **Keeling curve (SNAPSHOT).** Use the case pack's dated SNAPSHOT of the NOAA Mauna Loa monthly record, printed as a chart and as a table, labeled SNAPSHOT with its source and capture date. Students mark the 1958 value, a recent value, and pre-industrial 280 ppm. Why does the line zigzag each year? (Northern Hemisphere plants take in carbon in summer and release it in winter.) Note with students that the record has a gap in 2022 to 2023 and that some months in the file are filled estimates, as the file itself states [verify]. Hand twin: plot five or six table values by hand on graph paper and draw the curve through them.
3. **Connect.** Write, draw, or say three sentences linking the lab to one Tribal mitigation example, from the shared case or the Local lesson.

## Check for Understanding

1. Name the four places carbon is kept, and one way carbon moves between two of them. Say it, point to the containers, or draw it.
   Listen for: atmosphere, ocean, land and living things, fossil carbon; a flow such as photosynthesis, breathing out, the ocean taking in CO2, or burning fossil fuels.
2. Why does the Keeling curve zigzag every year, and why does it climb?
   Listen for: plants in the north take in carbon in summer and release it in winter; the climb is fossil carbon added faster than the circle takes it back.
3. What makes the change of the last 150 years different from ice-age changes?
   Listen for: speed (about 150 years, not thousands) and cause (fossil fuel burning and land clearing, not Earth's orbit).

## Instructor notes

## Sovereignty thread

Clean energy and consent bring all three rings into one question: who decides what is built on a Nation's lands and near its sacred places?

- **Ring 1, the Nation:** a Nation decides what mitigation looks like on its own lands, and it holds reserved rights that a project can affect. Mitigation done by a Nation is self-determination in action.
- **Ring 2, InterTribal:** Nations often take shared positions on energy siting and consultation through InterTribal organizations such as {{region.intertribal}} [verify]. Students read those positions in the organizations' own public words; no student speaks for one.
- **Ring 3, International:** the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples speaks to free, prior, and informed consent before projects on Indigenous lands [verify], and the Paris Agreement names Indigenous Peoples' knowledge in adaptation [verify].

Question to carry: When a clean energy project and a Nation's rights meet, what would meaningful consultation have to include? See the primer, "Putting the rings to work."
