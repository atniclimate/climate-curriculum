## Key lesson

Observation is a skill. People have always sensed change in their places; instruments add a record that can be compared across places and years.

## Key takeaway

Your senses and your instruments answer different questions. Put them side by side and each makes the other stronger.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Describe Indigenous approaches to ecological monitoring in general terms, and identify who in their own community holds that practice.
- Use basic instruments (thermometer, rain gauge, water thermometer, pH strips) and record data with metadata (who, where, when, how).
- Set up a photo point.
- Compare a personal observation with an official record.

## Hands-on activity

**Sensing Walk and Instrument Station.**

1. **Ten quiet minutes:** students spread out on campus or at a nearby creek, alone, without phones. They record what they hear, smell, see, and feel.
2. **Instrument station:** in teams, measure air temperature in sun and shade, water temperature if water is near, and set a rain gauge. Take a photo point: mark the spot, note the direction, photograph the same frame (or sketch it from the same spot).
3. **Metadata practice:** every data point gets who, where, when, and how. Discuss why a number without metadata is hard to trust, and why a missing reading is written "missing", never zero.
4. **Compare:** find the nearest weather station, United States Geological Survey (USGS) stream gauge, or snow site and compare. The ATNI Dynamic Drought Module (https://atniclimate.github.io/dynamic-drought-module/) maps public stations across the United States; its named regional views are mostly in the Pacific Northwest, so most sites start from its national view. The station's own agency page works too. Why might the numbers differ?
5. **Hand twin:** with no internet, compare against the printed station reading from the Week 2 local lesson (SNAPSHOT, dated). Every step above works with paper, a thermometer, and a pencil.

## Check for Understanding

1. Name one thing your senses noticed today that an instrument could not measure.
   Listen for: a sound, smell, color, feeling, or animal sign; anything real from the walk.
2. What four things should every data point carry so others can trust it?
   Listen for: who, where, when, and how.
3. Your reading and the station's reading were different. Give one reason why.
   Listen for: a different place, height, time, shade or sun, instrument, or a mistake that was noticed and explained.

## Instructor notes

Do not describe any Tribe's specific traditional monitoring practices unless a knowledge holder from that Tribe has shared them for this purpose. It is fine to say that Tribal Peoples have tracked seasons, water, and animal behavior for generations, and then invite a practitioner to speak for their own practice.

The drought module and agency pages are LIVE; check them outside class before relying on them, and keep the printed comparison ready.

## Sovereignty thread

When a Tribal program monitors its own water, air, or relatives, the record is that Nation's to share or hold (sovereignty primer, "Ring 1: The Nation").
