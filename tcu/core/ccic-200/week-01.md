## Key lesson

This course is meant to lead to mindful and practical action. It is going to take all of us, and that includes our relationships with our non-human relatives.

## Key takeaway

Everyone here already carries knowledge about climate and place. The work begins by honoring the Self, the People, and the Future, and by agreeing on how we will learn together.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Introduce themselves through their homeplace, Nation, and relationship to land and water.
- Co-create classroom norms and agreements.
- Describe the course's modular design and how learning will be assessed.
- Place their own weather and climate memories on a personal timeline.

## Hands-on activity

**Norms and the Half-Circle Timeline.**

- **Norms:** in small groups, students answer "What do I need from this group to learn well?" and "What will I give?" The class merges answers into five to eight agreements, posted all term. Include agreements on confidentiality of personal and community stories, and on care when topics get heavy.
- **Talking circle note:** the circle and a talking piece support equal voice. Permission from some Tribal leaders is not permission from all. If you want to use a talking stick or another cultural object, ask the local Tribe's cultural resources staff first; otherwise use a neutral object such as a stone or a pen.
- **Half-circle timeline** (adapted from Steve Robinson's *Climate Change and the Tribes: Columbia River Edition* draft): on a large sheet, draw the top half of a circle. From birth (left) to now (the top), place memories and milestones connected to weather, seasons, water, fire, harvest, and place. Drawings, words, and images all work. Students keep these; the second half comes back in Week 10.
- **Hand twin:** everything here is paper, markers, and talk. Nothing needs a screen or the internet.

## Check for Understanding

1. What are the four parts of every class session in this course? Say them, or draw one picture for each.
   Listen for: Presence, Application, Inquiry, and Reflection, in any order, with a few words on what each one means (showing up, doing, asking, looking back).

   Listen for: any real memory tied to weather or place; there is no wrong memory. A pass is fine.

   Listen for: the Columbia River Basin as the shared case, and their own Nation, homelands, or community as the center.

## Instructor notes

Students come from many Nations. Invite each to name their own Nation and place without asking anyone to speak for their Tribe. Some students carry recent losses from fire, flood, or heat; let them share as much as they choose.

The land acknowledgment belongs to the Nation. Until the Nation has written or approved one, leave the mark in place and say so aloud; an honest gap teaches more than borrowed words.

## Sovereignty thread

Climate work is sovereignty work: deciding what matters, what data to trust, who holds knowledge, and who decides. This week opens the sovereignty primer at "Ring 1: The Nation": each Nation is a government with its own lands, waters, laws, and knowledge, and only that Nation speaks for itself. That is why the land acknowledgment in the Local lesson waits for the Nation, and why no one in this room is asked to speak for their Tribe. Later weeks widen the view to "Ring 2: InterTribal" and "Ring 3: International."
