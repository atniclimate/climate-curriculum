## Key lesson

Climate change reaches people through their relatives: the water, fish, animals, roots, and berries that sustain them. When a First Food is stressed, a way of life is stressed.

## Key takeaway

Salmon are a measuring stick. Watching their water, their timing, and their returns tells us about the health of the whole system, including ourselves. Every place has relatives that can teach the same way.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Explain how warming, changing flow, and changing seasons affect traditional foods, wildlife, plants, and water.
- Describe the Columbia River salmon case (the shared case): historic abundance, dams, warm water, and the 2015 die-off.
- Read how a Tribal plan names cultural resources and the climate threats to them.

## Hands-on activity

**First Foods Impact Web.**

1. Start with CTUIR's published First Foods order as an example, as CTUIR publishes it: water, salmon, deer, cous, huckleberry. The order itself carries meaning that CTUIR defines; do not interpret it for them.
2. Each student builds a web for one relative from their own homelands (a fish, plant, animal, or water source), using only what they know or what is publicly shared by their Tribe. The local lesson's public species list can help a student who wants to start from this place. At the center: the relative. First ring: what it needs (cold water, snowmelt timing, fire, pollinators, flow). Second ring: the climate changes that affect those needs. Third ring: who depends on it, human and non-human.
3. Pairs trace one cascade out loud: for example, less snowpack leads to lower, warmer summer flow, which leads to thermal stress on adult salmon, which leads to fewer fish reaching spawning grounds and fewer fish for Treaty harvest.
4. **Hand twin:** the web is paper, string, or chalk. A student who would rather not draw tells the cascade aloud or writes it as a chain of sentences.

## Check for Understanding

1. Name one relative from your web and one thing it needs to stay well.
   Listen for: a real plant, animal, fish, or water, and a need such as cold water, the right timing, clean water, or room to move.
2. Tell or draw one chain of changes, from a warmer climate to that relative.
   Listen for: at least three linked steps that make sense in order, such as less snow, then lower and warmer water, then stressed fish.
3. Why does the class not explain what CTUIR's First Foods order means?
   Listen for: the meaning belongs to CTUIR to define; we read what a Nation publishes and do not speak for it.

## Instructor notes

Shared case: {{case.shared}}. Case points (each a verify row in the core):

- Historic returns are estimated at 10 to 16 million salmon and steelhead a year.
- The four CRITFC member Tribes (the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, the Confederated Tribes of the Warm Springs Reservation of Oregon, the Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation, and the Nez Perce Tribe) reserved fishing rights at usual and accustomed places in their 1855 Treaties; the rights were reserved, not granted (*U.S. v. Winans*, 1905). The 1846 Oregon Treaty was a boundary treaty between the United States and Great Britain, not the source of these rights.
- Celilo Falls was flooded by The Dalles Dam on March 10, 1957.
- Adult salmon migration is impaired above roughly 20 to 21 °C (68 to 70 °F). In 2015, roughly 250,000 adult sockeye died in warm Columbia Basin water. Steve Robinson's draft reports that the Nez Perce Tribe's current climate program started after the 2015 drought and fish kill [verify].
- For contrast and hope, the Elwha River: two dams (Elwha Dam, 1913; Glines Canyon Dam, 1927) were removed from 2011 to 2014, the largest dam removal in U.S. history at the time. The Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe's part in that story is the Tribe's to tell. [NEEDS TRIBAL CONFIRMATION: Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe] Film: PBS Nature, "The Beautiful Undammed" (Wild Hope): https://www.pbs.org/wnet/nature/the-beautiful-undammed-wild-hope/29052/

Local case: {{case.local}}. In the weeks ahead it is analyzed in full (Week 9); this week, let students notice which relatives it touches.

A student's web about their own family's foods belongs to that student; do not collect it or post it. Nothing written in class speaks for a Nation.

## Sovereignty thread

The shared case shows two rings at work. "Ring 1: The Nation": the Columbia River Treaty Tribes reserved their fishing rights; no one granted them. "Ring 2: InterTribal": the four Tribes also act together through an InterTribal commission, CRITFC.
