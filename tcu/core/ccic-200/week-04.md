## Key lesson

Climate is social as much as it is scientific; society moves action much more than the science. How we come to know things, and who we trust, shapes what we believe.

## Key takeaway

Narrative and emotion move us whether we notice it or not, so we learn to read both our sources and ourselves. Think about thinking.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Describe Indigenous perspectives on environmental change and intergenerational knowledge transfer.
- Compare how different knowledge systems (Indigenous Knowledges, Western science, local and intercultural knowledges) establish what is true.
- Evaluate a climate source for evidence, agenda, and emotional pull.
- Prepare a respectful, consent-based conversation with a knowledge holder.

## Hands-on activity

**Reading Sources and Ourselves.**

1. **Source and self mapping:** each student lists where they get climate information (family, Elders, social media, news, school, Tribal government). For each: Why do I trust it? What does it want me to feel? What evidence does it show? Groups compare maps. This works on paper: one sheet, one column per question.
2. **The Elder-and-child test:** in pairs, each student explains weather versus climate (from Week 2) in one minute, plain enough to enter and deep enough to respect, so that both an Elder and a child would understand. Partners listen as themselves and give one piece of feedback: what was clear, and what one word could be plainer.
3. **Interview practice:** using the Story of Self, Story of Us, Story of Now prompts (syllabus, Appendix A), students practice asking for consent and listening without interrupting. Each person speaks only as themselves, about their own life. Every partner may decline any question as themselves ("not that question"), and the asker practices answering with thanks and moving on. Then the class reads the scenario below and talks it through. Nobody plays an Elder or a knowledge holder.

   *Scenario (written for discussion):* A student has asked permission, explained the class, and offered a gift. Partway through, the person they are visiting says, "Not that question." The student had planned five more questions on the same topic. What should the student say next? What should they write in their reflection? What should they leave out?

## Check for Understanding

1. Pick one place you get climate news. Why do you trust it, or not?
   Listen for: a reason tied to evidence, to a relationship, or to a feeling; noticing the feeling counts.
2. What do you ask for before a conversation with a knowledge holder begins?
   Listen for: consent: who you are, why you are asking, what the class will see, and whether notes or recording are allowed.
3. A knowledge holder says "not now" to your request. Did you fail the assignment? What do you do?
   Listen for: no; thank them, respect the answer, write about the experience, and choose an alternative.

## Sovereignty thread

Knowledge is governed by the people and Nations it comes from. "Ring 1: The Nation" in the sovereignty primer explains why a Nation's protocols, review rules, and permissions decide who may ask an Elder what, and why everything shared stays at Tier 3 by default. "Ring 3: International" shows the same idea at a world scale: the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance, and how international climate bodies have come to treat Indigenous Knowledge. Neither ring gives permission; only the Nation and the person who shares can do that.
