## How to Use This Template

This is a complete, teachable 200-level course for Tribal Colleges and Universities (TCUs), with a syllabus here and a page for each week. The shared core is the same at every site; each campus adds its own place through local lessons and its own people through local experts.

- **What stays the same everywhere:** key lessons and takeaways, objectives, the nine NWIC catalog outcomes, assignments, the rubric, policies, the NESC 215 reflection prompts, and the shared case (the Columbia River Basin).
- **What each campus adds:** a local lesson in each week (a "socket"), a local case close to home, and local experts and knowledge holders, through the campus's and the Nation's own protocols.
- **Marks on the page:** a fact slot shows a public fact about the campus or region, or the word "unknown". A local expert box is room for local teaching. A Tribal confirmation box is something only that Nation can supply or approve. A placeholder is for the institution or instructor; an "instructor selects" box is a reading the instructor chooses; a verify tag is a detail to confirm before teaching. A mark is never replaced with a guess.
- **Where CCIC 200 stops and TCR 201 starts:** CCIC 200 is an early primer, not a technical or data-science course: weather and climate, local impacts, ways of knowing, and sovereignty from the Nation through InterTribal and international policy, ending in an introductory adaptation plan outline. TCR 201 carries that outline into action: leadership engagement, policy, funding, and implementation.

## Course Description

Climate change is not a distant forecast in Indian Country. It is already here: in rivers that run too warm for salmon in August, in smoke that settles over camp and classroom, in snow that falls as rain, in First Foods that ripen early or not at all. This course starts where understanding starts, with experience. It begins with a cloud, because everyone knows what one is, and works outward to the atmosphere, the carbon cycle, and the gauges and data points that tell a story of change over time, not on speculation but on momentum.

Climate is social as much as it is scientific. So the course also asks how people come to know what they know, whose knowledge they trust, and why Indigenous Knowledges and Western science see farther together than either sees alone. Students keep a weather journal, observe a place over weeks, sit with an Elder or knowledge holder and listen, read a real Tribal climate adaptation plan, analyze local climate trends, and draft the outline of an adaptation plan for a concern in their own community.

Sovereignty is the organizing principle. Climate work is sovereignty work: deciding what matters, what data to trust, who holds knowledge, and who decides. The course follows sovereignty outward in three rings: the Nation, Nations acting together, and the international table. The lands, waters, and Beings who live within them are relatives, not resources. The Columbia River Basin, with its salmon, its Treaty Tribes, and its dams, is the shared case study. Beside it, {{institution.name}} sets a local case close to campus, {{case.local}}, and every student also centers their own Nation and homelands, wherever they are.

The surest sign of understanding is being able to pass it on. By the end, each student should be able to explain climate change so that both an Elder and a child understand, and carry a working plan outline forward into TCR 201, their community, and their career.

## Course Overview

This 10-week course examines climate change impacts, perceptions, and adaptation strategies in American Indian and Alaska Native communities, with a focus on developing practical climate adaptation planning skills. Through multiple ways of knowing, students explore how climate has changed in the past, how it is changing now, the contemporary causes of climate change, and what changes are to come. The course is designed for Tribal College and University students from many Nations: the Columbia River Basin serves as a shared case, each campus adds a local case, and each student applies the learning to their own Tribe, homelands, and community.

## Course Learning Objectives

1. **Understand** weather and climate patterns affecting Tribal lands.
2. **Apply** local sensing techniques to document environmental changes.
3. **Analyze** climate data and projections relevant to Tribal Nations.
4. **Evaluate** climate hazards and vulnerabilities specific to Tribal communities.
5. **Understand** climate adaptation strategies for Tribal Nations.
6. **Connect** with knowledge holders and practitioners engaged in climate adaptation in your Tribe or community.

## Outcomes (NWIC Catalog)

These nine outcomes come from the Northwest Indian College catalog for NESC 215 [verify]. Students who complete the course will be able to:

1. **Apply** basic concepts of meteorology, climatology, and Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) to explain and understand their local climate and environment.
2. **Summarize** how Earth's climate system works, and understand the physical processes and dynamic interactions of the atmosphere, oceans, ice, and land surface.
3. **Describe** changes in climate through time, and distinguish between long-term geologic-scale climate change and recent human-caused climate change.
4. **Discuss** current impacts of climate change on humans and ecosystems, with emphasis on impacts to Coast Salish Tribes and other Native Peoples. [Instructor note: outside NWIC, name the Nations of {{region.name}} alongside Coast Salish Tribes, each by the name it uses for itself.]
5. **Illustrate** specific examples of climate change impacts on plants, animals, land, or water resources in your Tribal community.
6. **Discuss** Peoples' unique adaptations to their place, and demonstrate examples of how TEK and intergenerational knowledge of the natural world can assist in solving environmental issues.
7. **Describe** various approaches to collecting and analyzing data, including field data collection, visual data analysis, and using data to understand trends or discover underlying issues.
8. **Apply** scientific knowledge and TEK toward positive solutions to the impacts of climate change while respecting Tribal values and strengthening community ties.
9. **Describe** the global carbon cycle and the role of carbon dioxide (CO2) in creating a greenhouse effect, including the recent (100-year) changes in the composition of the atmosphere and its role in global warming.

## Course Design

### Two Grammars, One Week

Both grammars come from Patrick Freeland's own course syllabi.

1. **Session shape (NESC 215, Northwest Indian College):** "Presence, Application (Assignments), Inquiry, & Reflection." Every class session moves through these four modules. Presence is showing up for each other; Application is hands-on work; Inquiry is questions that lead to more questions; Reflection is writing or speaking about what shifted.
2. **Module record (College of the Muscogee Nation course syllabi, 2022 and 2023):** each module "consists of four parts": attending the in-class lecture or presentation, an in-class Check for Understanding, a module assignment, and a reflection, 25 points each, 100 points per module.

In this course, Presence includes the opening circle, check-in, going outside, and a guest or Elder when one is available; it is where relationships are built, and it is graded as participation, not performance. Every week has a hands-on activity, a key lesson, and a reflection prompt carried from NESC 215.

| Session shape (time) | Module record (evidence) | What the week page carries |
|---|---|---|
| Presence | Attending the lecture or presentation | Opening, guest or Elder slot, going outside |
| Inquiry | Check for Understanding | Key lesson; three short Check for Understanding items, answered in class, said, drawn, or written |
| Application | Module assignment | The hands-on activity and the assignment step due this week |
| Reflection | Reflection | The week's reflection prompt (NESC 215 prompts kept) and one "say it plain" line: the week's idea in words both an Elder and a child could understand |

Checks for Understanding are feedback first: a wrong answer is a teaching moment, not a penalty.

### The Four Questions: Understanding a Climate of Change

The four-part series by Patrick Freeland and Cayla Jones, "Understanding a Climate of Change: Understanding an Ever Changing Climate," gives the course its arc. Each part's key lesson and key takeaway appear in the weeks it anchors.

| Part | Core Idea | CCIC 200 Weeks |
|---|---|---|
| Part 1: How Does a Cloud Work? | Weather is physics; the same gauges that predict tomorrow show change over years. | Weeks 2 and 3 |
| Part 2: How Do You Know What You Know? | Climate is social as much as scientific; read your sources and yourself. | Weeks 4 and 6 |
| Part 3: What Can We Do? (Adaptation, Mitigation, Resilience) | Three different jobs with different tools; the impacts are already here. | Weeks 7, 8, and 9 |
| Part 4: How Do We Work Together? (Relationships Matter) | It takes all of us, including our non-human relatives; be a good ancestor. | Weeks 1, 4, and 10 |

Week 5 (Ecosystem Impacts) is the bridge between Part 2 and Part 3: it moves from how we know change to what change is doing to First Foods, plants, animals, and water.

### Three Eyes

Every week names the scale of the evidence in front of students:

- **Frog's Eye:** up close, at the water. The campus, a creek or shoreline, a rain gauge, a field site: what a person can see, hear, and measure on foot.
- **Bird's Eye:** above the land. The watershed ({{place.watershed_huc8}}) and ecoregion ({{place.ecoregion_l3}}), the county ({{place.county}}) and the climate unit ({{place.climate_unit}}), the hazards, the plans, and the partners.
- **Moon's Eye:** the whole planet. Global records, satellites, and the large patterns that reach this place.

Every eye has a blank it cannot see past. The frog cannot see the watershed; the moon cannot see the creek. Each week says which eye it uses so students know what the evidence can and cannot show.

### The Shared Case and the Local Case

TCUs serve students from many Nations. The course uses one shared case so that everyone can reason together, adds a local case close to campus, and asks every student to carry each lesson home.

- **Shared case: {{case.shared}}.** Salmon, the four Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC) member Tribes (Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation (CTUIR), Confederated Tribes of the Warm Springs Reservation of Oregon, Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakama Nation, and Nez Perce Tribe), Treaty-reserved rights, dams, warming water, snowpack loss, and Tribal leadership in restoration and climate planning.
- **Local case: {{case.local}}.** A public, place-based climate story near campus, written from public agency sources, never speaking for a Nation. It appears in the weekly local lessons and is analyzed in full in Week 9.
- **Your homelands:** in every assignment, students may center their own Tribe and place. A student from the Plains, the Southwest, Alaska, the Great Lakes, Oklahoma, California, or the Northwest does the same work for home.
- **Local lessons:** each week has one socket where the campus adds its own place-based lesson. An unfilled socket stays visible as an open invitation to local experts.
- **Boundaries:** students never present Treaty, ceded, or Usual and Accustomed boundaries as definitive jurisdiction without the Nation's own maps and words.

### The Sovereignty Thread

CCIC 200 carries a short sovereignty primer (a separate page) in three rings, and each week points to the ring it touches:

1. **Ring 1: The Nation.** Sovereignty, reserved rights, the trust responsibility, and self-determination, in plain words.
2. **Ring 2: InterTribal.** How Nations act together through InterTribal organizations, resolutions, consultation, and shared positions. In this region: {{region.intertribal}}.
3. **Ring 3: International.** The international layer: Indigenous Peoples' rights, the climate convention, how international science treats Indigenous Knowledge, and the CARE Principles.

Nothing in the primer speaks for any Nation or organization. TCR 201 puts the rings to work.

### Meeting Pattern Assumption

The week pages assume **one 2-hour (120-minute) session per week** on a 10-week quarter, matching NWIC. For **two 75-minute sessions per week** (150 minutes), use this split:

- **Session A (75 min):** Presence, Inquiry, and the first part of Application.
- **Session B (75 min):** the rest of Application, the Check for Understanding, Reflection, and the extra 30 minutes for field time, the local lesson, guest conversation, or assignment studio.

### Weeks 7 and 8: The Design Choice

The NESC 215 source jumps from Week 6 to Week 9. CCIC 200 fills the gap this way:

- **Week 7: Climate Hazards and Risks Where We Live.** Heat, drought, wildfire and smoke, flooding, snowpack loss, and ocean and ice change where they apply, with hazard cascades. The shared case brings the Northwest's hazards; the local lesson brings this region's. This gives Week 9 (Risk, Impact, and Vulnerability) the hazard knowledge it needs.
- **Week 8: The Carbon Cycle, Climate Through Time, and Mitigation Science.** This week teaches NWIC Outcome 9 (the global carbon cycle and CO2), which no NESC week teaches explicitly, and strengthens Outcome 3 (geologic versus recent climate change). It also gives Part 3 its mitigation week, so Weeks 7 to 9 read as adaptation context, mitigation, and resilience planning.

Social vulnerability, health, and First Foods are not dropped: First Foods anchor Week 5, and health and social vulnerability anchor Week 9.

## Assignments

Each assignment lets students center their own Tribe and homelands. Where an assignment involves Tribal knowledge, the rules in "Data Sovereignty and Data Care" apply.

### 1. Weather Journal (Weeks 2 to 9)

**Purpose:** to build the habit of observing weather as physics you can see and measure, and to notice how descriptive language tells a story.

**What to do:**
- Make at least five entries per week, at roughly the same time of day when you can.
- Each entry records: date, time, location; sky and cloud types; temperature (felt, and measured if you have a thermometer); wind (direction and strength in your own words); precipitation; and one thing living Beings are doing (birds, plants, insects, fish, people).
- Once a week, compare one entry with the nearest official record: a National Weather Service station, a USGS stream gauge, a snow site where one is near, or a Community Collaborative Rain, Hail and Snow Network (CoCoRaHS) observer. The Week 2 local lesson names the nearest station; the agency's own site or the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module (see Appendix D for what it covers) can find others.
- If you carry words for weather in your own language and choose to use them, you may. No one is expected to, and no one should borrow another Nation's words without permission.

**Deliverable:** the journal (paper, sketchbook, or digital) and a one-page summary at Week 9: What patterns did you see? What surprised you? What did the official record miss, and what did you miss?

**Grading notes:** graded on consistency, attention, and growth in observation, not on artistic quality or spelling. Check-ins at Weeks 4 and 7 are feedback only. Sketches count as entries.

### 2. Field Observation Project (Assigned Week 3, Due Week 6)

**Purpose:** to combine local sensing with instrumental observation at one place over time.

**What to do:**
- Choose one site you can visit safely at least three times between Weeks 3 and 6: a creek, shoreline, garden, fishing site, schoolyard, or your own yard. The Week 3 local lesson lists public sites near campus. If the site is culturally sensitive, do not record its exact location in anything you turn in.
- **Local sensing:** at each visit, spend ten quiet minutes using all your senses. Record sounds, smells, color, water level and clarity, plant stage, animal signs, and what people nearby say about the place.
- **Instrumental observation:** at each visit, take at least two measurements (air temperature, water temperature, rain gauge reading, pH strip, turbidity by eye or with a tube) and one fixed-point photograph (or sketch) from the same spot and angle.
- **Comparison:** pull the matching official record for your visit days (nearest weather station, USGS gauge, or snow site) from the agency's site or the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module.

**Deliverable:** field notes from all visits, the photo-point series, a simple data table, and a two-page summary: What did your senses tell you that the instruments did not? Where did they agree? What would you monitor next, and for how long?

**Grading notes:** 7 percent of the grade. Weighted toward the quality of observation and the honesty of the comparison. Missing or messy measurements are fine if explained; a missing reading is written "missing", never zero. Optional extension: register as a CoCoRaHS observer and keep reporting after the course.

### 3. Listen and Learn: A Conversation With an Elder or Knowledge Holder (Assigned Week 4, Due Week 9)

**Purpose:** to learn from intergenerational knowledge about environmental change, and to practice doing that respectfully.

**What to do:**
1. **Find the right path in.** Ask through your family, your Tribe's cultural resources or education department, or the instructor. The Week 4 local lesson describes the path at {{institution.name}}. Some Tribes require approval before a student interviews an Elder for a class; follow your Tribe's protocols.
2. **Ask for consent before the conversation.** Explain who you are, why you are asking, what the class will see, and what it will not. Ask whether you may take notes or record. Make it clear the knowledge holder may decline any question, stop at any time, or withdraw everything later. Use the consent note in Appendix B, or the Tribe's own form if it has one.
3. **Offer compensation or a gift.** Follow the knowledge holder's and the community's customs. The institution provides [PLACEHOLDER: honorarium amount, gift budget, and how students request it]. Students do not pay out of pocket.
4. **Listen.** Use the Story of Self, Story of Us, Story of Now prompts in Appendix A as a guide, not a script. Ask about changes they have seen in weather, water, plants, animals, and seasons over their life, and what they would tell the next generation.
5. **Care for what you were given.** Treat everything shared as Tier 3 (sovereign) by default: it stays with you and the knowledge holder. Offer to share your notes back. Return or delete recordings as agreed.

**Deliverable:** a three- to four-page reflection on what you learned about change and about listening, written so it contains nothing the knowledge holder has not cleared for the class. Attach a signed or recorded consent note (kept by the instructor, not shared) and a short "what may be shared" note that says which parts, if any, the knowledge holder released for class discussion.

**Grading notes:** 8 percent of the grade. Graded on respect for process, quality of listening, and reflection, never on how much knowledge was "collected." A student who asks respectfully and is told "not now" has done the assignment well; they write about that experience and complete an alternative (a public talk by a Tribal knowledge holder, a Tribe-produced oral history, or an in-class Elder visit). Students never publish or post knowledge from this assignment.

### 4. Cultural Resource Impacts Review (Assigned Week 5, Due Week 7)

**Purpose:** to read a real Tribal climate adaptation plan (CAP) or vulnerability assessment the way a planner does, and see how a Tribe names its cultural resources and the climate threats to them.

**What to do:**
- Choose one public plan from those the instructor selects [INSTRUCTOR SELECTS], or your own Tribe's public plan. Examples from the shared case region: the Lummi Nation, CTUIR, or Swinomish Indian Tribal Community plans. Find plans through the Tribal Resilience Actions database (https://tribalresilienceactions.org/plans/), the Tribal Climate Change Guide (https://tribalclimateguide.uoregon.edu/), or the Tribe's own website. The CTUIR Climate Adaptation Plan executive summary is at https://ctuir.org/media/oeklm2el/cap-executive-summary-final-std-pg-no.pdf .
- Build a table: cultural resource named in the plan; climate hazard(s) linked to it; how the plan rates or describes vulnerability; strategies proposed; gaps you notice.
- Note how the plan frames its values in its own words (for example, CTUIR's First Foods order as CTUIR publishes it: water, salmon, deer, cous, huckleberry). Quote; do not interpret a Nation's values for it.

**Deliverable:** the table and a two- to three-page review: What does the plan protect first, and why? How does it bring together science and TEK? What is missing or unclear? What would you ask the plan's authors?

**Grading notes:** 7 percent. Use only what the plan publishes. Critique respectfully: a plan reflects a Nation's decisions, capacity, and moment in time. Students may not have access to non-public Tribal plans; they should not ask for them for a class assignment.

### 5. Local Climate Trend Analysis (Assigned Week 6, Due Week 7)

**Purpose:** to read historical climate records and downscaled projections for your homelands, and explain them plainly.

**What to do:**
- Start from the Week 6 local lesson: the climate profile for {{place.climate_unit}}, a dated SNAPSHOT with its scenario, period, and baseline named.
- Where it covers your region, use The Climate Toolbox (https://climatetoolbox.org/), including the Tribal Climate Tool, for one location in your homelands. The Climate Toolbox describes itself as covering the contiguous United States; students in Alaska and Hawaii use the climate profile and their regional climate center or climate adaptation science center ({{region.casc}}) instead.
- Add one Columbia River Basin location for the shared case: a public station or a basin point, not a reservation. A chart of a Nation's lands is public data about that Nation, not its voice; chart a reservation only for your own Nation's lands or with that Nation's confirmation.
- Pull at least three variables that matter to a concern you care about (for example: summer maximum temperature, number of very hot days, April 1 snow water equivalent, annual or seasonal precipitation, stream flow).
- Compare the historical baseline with mid-century projections under a lower and a higher emissions scenario.
- Set this year's conditions (drought, streamflow, snowpack, heat) beside the long-term picture, using the ATNI Dynamic Drought Module (https://atniclimate.github.io/dynamic-drought-module/) or your state climatologist's public pages ({{region.state_climatologist}}).

**Deliverable:** three labeled charts or screenshots, each marked LIVE or SNAPSHOT with its source and date, and each projection marked "projection, not a prediction" with its scenario, period, and baseline; a two-page memo (what changed, what is projected, how certain, what it means for your concern); and one paragraph written so that both an Elder and a child could understand it.

**Grading notes:** 7 percent. Weighted toward correct reading of the charts, honest statements about uncertainty, and the plain-language paragraph.

### 6. Carbon Cycle Lab (In Class, Week 8)

**Purpose:** to describe the global carbon cycle and the recent change in atmospheric CO2 (NWIC Outcome 9).

**Deliverable:** a completed lab sheet with the Keeling curve plot and interpretation, a labeled carbon cycle diagram, and three sentences connecting it to one Tribal mitigation example. Details are on the Week 8 page.

**Grading notes:** 3 percent. Graded for completion and accuracy of the core ideas. Students who miss class complete it with the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) data online or a printed copy of the monthly table.

### 7. Framing Worksheet (Part 1 in Week 7, Part 2 in Week 8, Due Week 8)

**Purpose:** to scaffold the adaptation plan outline. This worksheet comes from American Indian Higher Education Consortium (AIHEC) climate resilience research mentorship with TCU student researchers.

**What to do:** first, name your issue (water, a First Food, crops, a road, a town, a river). Then fill in five columns:

| What's Known | What's Under Threat | Projected Impacts | Methods | Mitigation (and Adaptation) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Put your data here first: observations, measurements, maps, records, what knowledge holders shared and cleared. | Health, land, water, economy, community, ways of life, relatives. | What the projections say (from your Trend Analysis and regional sources). | How you would find out more: testing, monitoring, records, conversations. | What can be done to offset climate impacts? Sort each idea: adaptation (living with change) or mitigation (reducing the cause). |

- **Part 1 (Week 7):** What's Known, What's Under Threat, Projected Impacts.
- **Part 2 (Week 8):** Methods and Mitigation (and Adaptation).

**Deliverable:** the completed worksheet with sources noted.

**Grading notes:** 4 percent. A full, honest worksheet with gaps named is better than a polished one that hides them. An unknown is written "unknown", never left blank or zero.

### 8. Climate Adaptation Plan Outline (Assigned Week 9, Due Start of Week 10)

**Purpose:** to turn the framing worksheet into the outline of an adaptation plan for one priority concern.

**Outline sections:**
1. **Community and place:** whose plan this would be; the rights holders, relatives, and partners; the place and its relatives.
2. **Priority concern and values:** the concern, and the values that make it a priority (for example, a First Food, a fishing site, drinking water, Elders' health in heat).
3. **Climate trends and projections:** from your Trend Analysis.
4. **Vulnerability:** exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity.
5. **Goals and candidate strategies:** each labeled adaptation or mitigation.
6. **Knowledge, data, and data care:** what is needed; who holds it; how it will be protected (tier).
7. **Engagement:** who must be at the table, and how (rights holder engagement, knowledge holders, Tribal departments, youth).
8. **Monitoring:** three to five indicators you would track.
9. **Next steps:** what TCR 201 would add (implementation, funding, leadership engagement).

**Deliverable:** a three- to five-page outline.

## Assessment Criteria

Final products are evaluated on:

## Final Product Rubric

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Exemplary (4) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Understanding of climate science concepts** | Key concepts missing or confused (weather and climate, greenhouse effect, projections). | Core concepts present but with errors or unexplained jargon. | Concepts accurate and connected to the concern; uncertainty stated. | Concepts accurate, clearly linked from global to local, and explained so an Elder and a child could follow. |
| **Ethical integration of TEK / IKS** | Tribal knowledge absent, generic, or used without permission. | Knowledge referenced but loosely, or without clear attribution or consent. | Knowledge included only with permission, attributed as agreed, and treated as a way of knowing, not decoration. | Knowledge and science are woven with care; consent, tier, and protocol are explicit; the product strengthens relationships. |
| **Practicality of proposed solutions** | Solutions vague or unrelated to the identified impacts. | Solutions related but general, or unclear whether adaptation or mitigation. | Solutions specific, matched to impacts, and labeled adaptation or mitigation. | Solutions specific, sequenced, and grounded in examples that have worked in Tribal communities. |
| **Quality of analysis and presentation** | Little use of data; sources missing. | Data used, but charts unclear or conclusions overstated. | Data read correctly, sources cited, presentation organized and clear. | Data and observation combined persuasively; visuals clear; the story of change is easy to follow. |
| **Grounded consideration of community needs and resources** | Community absent, or described from the outside. | Community named, but needs and strengths assumed. | Needs, strengths, and existing capacity identified from real sources. | Community voice (with permission) shapes priorities; rights holders and relatives are central. |
| **Feasibility of implementation strategies** | No sense of who, when, or with what resources. | Some steps named; resources and roles unclear. | Realistic roles, timeline, resources, and indicators. | Realistic and adaptive: names risks, fallback options, and what TCR 201 or the Tribe would need to carry it forward. |

## Course Policies

These policies are written for TCU students, many of whom are parents, workers, caregivers, fishers, harvesters, and active participants in ceremonial and community life. They assume that learning happens in relationship, and that a student's obligations to family and community are part of who they are, not an interruption.

### Presence and Attendance

- **Presence matters more than seat time.** Come when you can, and communicate when you cannot.
- **Family and ceremonial obligations** are legitimate absences. You do not need to explain ceremonial matters in detail; a short message is enough.
- **Seasonal harvest, hunting, gathering, and fishing** (including Treaty fishing seasons) are legitimate absences when you communicate before or soon after. Tell the instructor early in the term if you know your seasons.
- **Bereavement, funerals, and community emergencies** (fire, flood, smoke, storms, evacuations) are excused.
- **Make-up path:** for any excused absence, complete a short make-up (read the week page, do the activity where possible, and write the reflection). Presence is not reduced for excused absences.

### Deadlines and Flexibility

- Deadlines keep the class moving together; they are not traps. Ask for an extension before the deadline when you can, and after it when you cannot. Most requests will be granted.
- **Resubmission:** you may revise and resubmit any assignment (except the final) once, within two weeks of feedback.

### Skills-Based, Not Only Time-Based, Learning

TCU student researchers in AIHEC's climate resilience mentorship named the difference between time-based and skills-based learning, and found better outcomes with hands-on learning than with sitting and looking at a book. This course follows that lesson:

- Assignments are judged on what you can do and explain, not on hours logged.
- Every week includes hands-on work.
- If you already have a skill (for example, you have monitored water quality for your Tribe), talk to the instructor about showing it another way.
- If you are willing to fail at something, you will figure it out. Mistakes in fieldwork and data are expected and welcome when you explain them honestly.

### Climate Maturity and Emotional Care

Climate change carries grief, fear, and anger, especially when it touches home. Students may step out of a heavy discussion at any time. You may choose a different case study if a topic is too close. The class practices climate maturity: facing hard futures honestly while taking care of ourselves and each other. ## Working With Tribes and Knowledge Holders Respectfully

This course asks students and instructors to learn from Tribal Nations and knowledge holders. That is a privilege with responsibilities.

- **Consult the local Tribe.** Invite knowledge holders through the Tribe's education, cultural resources, or natural resources department, and follow the Tribe's protocols for guests, recording, and sharing.
- **Permission from some is not permission from all.** Permission from some Tribal leaders or one Tribe does not extend to others. When in doubt, ask again, and ask the right people. No response is not consent.
- **Compensate knowledge holders.** Offer honoraria, gifts, travel, and meals according to local custom. Budget for this before the term starts.
- **Do not invent or borrow cultural content.** This template leaves Tribal teachings, stories, place names, and ceremonies to the Tribes. Instructors should too.
- **No one speaks as a Nation.** Students analyze and quote a Nation's own public words; no student speaks or acts as a Nation, council, Elder, or knowledge holder, in class or in an assignment.
- **Treaty and ceded boundaries.** Do not present Treaty, ceded, or Usual and Accustomed boundaries as definitive jurisdiction without the Nation's own maps and words.
- **Treaty rights are reserved, not granted.** The Tribes retained rights they already held (*U.S. v. Winans*, 1905). In the shared case, *U.S. v. Washington* (1974, the Boldt decision) affirmed a 50 percent share of harvestable fish, and *U.S. v. Oregon* (Belloni, 1969) continues through ongoing management agreements. In every region, the reserved rights are each Nation's to describe.
- **Label knowledge.** Local Contexts (https://localcontexts.org/) provides Traditional Knowledge (TK) and Biocultural (BC) Labels. Indigenous communities create and apply these labels themselves; respect any label you see, and never apply one on a community's behalf.
- **Follow the CARE Principles, and learn from OCAP®** (see the next section).
- **Names matter.** Use each Nation's own name for itself. In the shared case, use the correct name "Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC)."
- **Tribes are governments.** Engagement with Tribal Nations is government-to-government; Tribal citizens are rights holders and relatives, and should be named that way.

## Data Sovereignty and Data Care

Data about Tribal Peoples, lands, waters, and relatives belongs under the authority of the Nations it comes from. Data literacy is sovereignty; so is data care.

- **CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance** (Global Indigenous Data Alliance): **C**ollective benefit, **A**uthority to control, **R**esponsibility, and **E**thics. See Carroll, S. R., et al. (2020), "The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance," *Data Science Journal* 19.
- **OCAP®** (First Nations Information Governance Centre): **O**wnership, **C**ontrol, **A**ccess, and **P**ossession of First Nations data by First Nations. OCAP® is a First Nations framework, not a general rule for U.S. Tribes; it is taught here as an example of a Nation-led data standard.
- **Tiered Sovereign Data Framework (TSDF):** ATNI's four-tier approach to putting these principles into practice, using a watershed metaphor.

| Tier | Watershed Name | Meaning | In This Course |
|---|---|---|---|
| T0 | Rain | Open: released for public benefit by the Nation's own decision. | Public data (weather stations, gauges, published plans) and anything a Nation has chosen to publish. |
| T1 | Lake | Network: shared among Indigenous network members through reciprocal protocols. | Rarely used in class; only with the Nation's direction. |
| T2 | River | Negotiated: shared with outside partners through formal agreements. | Not used in class without a formal agreement. |
| T3 | Canoe | Sovereign: culturally sensitive; stays under the community's complete control. | The default for anything from a knowledge holder. It stays with the student and the knowledge holder. |

**Default rule:** if you are not sure which tier something belongs to, treat it as T3. Over-protecting can be corrected; under-protecting may cause harm that cannot be undone.

**Student practices:**
- Keep Listen and Learn notes and recordings private; return or delete them as agreed.
- Do not post, publish, or upload Tribal knowledge from this course, including to AI tools.
- Do not record exact locations of culturally sensitive sites in assignments.
- When you cite a knowledge holder, cite them only as they agreed.
- Treat a missing number as unknown, never zero, and say how one would find out.
- Respect people's fear of how data could reflect on them. A TCU student researcher learned that farmers declined to share soil results because they worried the data would reflect badly on them; participants need to know what you are doing, in a way they understand.

**ATNI Climate tools and data:** ATNI's tools support Tribal decisions; they never replace them. The Dynamic Drought Module is public (T0). Its Tribal Lands and Reservation Boundaries layers are public federal representations (from the U.S. Census Bureau and the Bureau of Indian Affairs), not definitive depictions of jurisdiction, and its two deployer slots for Tribal lands and Treaty areas ship empty so each Nation can fill its own copy with its own authorized data. ATNI-GeoBase is a harmonized public baseline (T0) that Tribes layer their own data on and keep sovereign. Access to non-public tools: [PLACEHOLDER: current access contact for ATNI Climate tools].

## Appendix A: Story of Self, Story of Us, Story of Now Conversation Guide

Adapted from the AIHEC climate resilience research mentorship protocol for use in the Listen and Learn assignment. Use it as a guide, not a script. Let the knowledge holder lead.

**Story of Self**
- Who are you? Where are you from?
- What is important to you? Who taught you to care about the land and water?
- What changes have you seen in weather, seasons, water, plants, or animals over your life?

**Story of Us**
- How has your family or community noticed and talked about these changes?
- What has your community done in response? Who does the work?
- How do people share what they know with each other and with young people?

**Story of Now**
- Considering all you have seen, what would you tell the next generation?
- What gives you hope? What worries you?
- What should people like me be careful about when we learn and share this kind of knowledge?
