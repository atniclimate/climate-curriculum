## Key lesson

Climate impacts are happening now, so we must adapt now if we are to be resilient. Vulnerability is not a trait of a people; it comes from exposure, sensitivity, and the capacity a community has to respond, much of which history and policy have shaped.

## Key takeaway

A plan is a set of relationships written down. Good adaptation planning starts with what a community values, uses the best data and knowledge it can trust, and names who decides.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Explain risk, impact, and vulnerability (exposure, sensitivity, adaptive capacity).
- Describe climate adaptation planning frameworks and Indigenous approaches to adaptation.
- Identify economic, health, and community considerations, including heat, smoke, and First Foods access.
- Describe rights holder engagement strategies.
- Draft an adaptation plan outline for one priority concern.

**Content notes.**

- **A common planning cycle:** scope the concern and values; gather data and knowledge; assess vulnerability; set goals; choose strategies; implement; monitor and adjust. Tribal plans adapt this cycle to their own governance and values.
- **Indigenous approaches:** plans organized around First Foods (CTUIR) or community-defined health indicators (the Swinomish Indian Tribal Community's work on Indigenous health indicators) put relationships and values at the center, not only infrastructure [verify]. Read these in each Nation's own published words.
- **Health and social vulnerability:** heat and smoke hit Elders, children, outdoor workers, and people without cooling first; loss of First Foods affects health and culture together.
- **Rights holder engagement:** Tribal citizens are rights holders, not an interest group. Government-to-government consultation is a legal and relational duty.
- **Shared case: {{case.shared}}, the Columbia River Treaty.** Tribes and First Nations were excluded from the original 1964 Treaty; U.S. Tribes have participated in the review since 2012, and the Syilx Okanagan, Ktunaxa, and Secwepemc Nations were observers on the Canadian delegation [verify]. The Robinson draft quotes a Tribal leader on being "left out of dinners only to be invited to dessert" [verify]. The Treaty's current status changes; check and date it before teaching [verify]. Negotiation status and policy strategy belong to TCR 201.

## Hands-on activity

**Vulnerability Matrix and Plan Skeleton.** Paper first; works with no network.

1. From your Framing Worksheet, pick your priority concern.
2. Build a matrix by hand: rows are the parts of the concern (for example, for a fishing site: water temperature, access road, drying racks, people who fish, the fish). Columns are exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity, rated low, medium, or high with one line of evidence each. A student may build it on the board with sticky notes, or say ratings aloud while a partner writes.
3. Practice once on the local case (Local lesson) as a class, then on your own concern.
4. Turn the highest-rated rows into draft goals and strategies, each labeled adaptation or mitigation.
5. Sketch the plan outline headings (see the syllabus) and fill in what you have. You are drafting, not deciding for a Tribe.
6. **Plan Assessor demonstration (optional):** the instructor shows a printed, dated capture of ATNI's Plan Assessor reading a public Tribal climate adaptation plan: a rubric-based reading, not a grade, showing what is present, what is missing, and where the plan's priorities align or diverge from federal program requirements. Use only a plan the Nation published, and link the plan rather than summarize it. `[PLACEHOLDER: current access contact for ATNI Climate tools]`

## Check for Understanding

1. Name the three parts of vulnerability, with one example of each for one concern. Say, draw, or write it.
   Listen for: exposure (how much the hazard reaches it), sensitivity (how much it is hurt), adaptive capacity (what it has to respond); examples tied to one concern.
2. Why is vulnerability "not a trait of a people"?
   Listen for: it comes from exposure, sensitivity, and capacity, which history and policy shaped; it can change, and it says nothing about who people are.
3. Who decides a Tribe's adaptation plan, and what is a student's role in drafting one?
   Listen for: the Nation decides; rights holders are engaged government to government; a student drafts and asks questions, and does not decide for a Tribe.

## Sovereignty thread

Rights holders, not interest groups: this week puts Ring 1 and Ring 2 of the primer side by side, and the shared case reaches into Ring 3.

- **Ring 1, the Nation:** a Nation's adaptation plan is a decision of a government. Treaty rights are reserved by the Tribes, not granted to them (*U.S. v. Winans*, 1905), and the federal trust responsibility and government-to-government consultation follow from that relationship.
- **Ring 2, InterTribal:** Nations coordinate on shared waters and fish through InterTribal bodies; in the shared case, the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC). In this region: {{region.intertribal}}. Read their positions in their own public words.
- **Ring 3, International:** the Columbia River Treaty is an agreement between two countries over a river that Tribes and First Nations relied on long before the border, and that left them out at the start [verify].

Questions to carry: Who was at the table when the plan for your concern was made? Who should be? See the primer, "Questions to carry."
