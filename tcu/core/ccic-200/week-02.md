## Key lesson

Weather is based on fundamental physics, here and throughout the universe. Pressure and water are always moving, never fixed or stuck at one height.

## Key takeaway

The same physics, and the same gauges and data points that predict tomorrow's weather, also show us patterns and change over many years. With enough data we can look ahead to what may come, not on speculation but on momentum.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Distinguish weather (the state of the atmosphere now) from climate (weather averaged over about 30 years or more).
- Explain how pressure, water, and heat make a cloud, rain, and wind.
- Describe local weather and climate patterns through more than one way of knowing.
- Name at least two climate impacts already documented on Tribal lands.

## Hands-on activity

**How Does a Cloud Work?**

1. **Cloud in a bottle:** a clear plastic bottle, a little warm water, and a small amount of smoke (or a hand pump). Squeeze and release: pressure drops, air cools, water vapor condenses on particles, and a cloud appears. Students write down what they saw before explaining it.
2. **earth.nullschool (LIVE):** in pairs, students find a low-pressure system and track wind and moisture toward the Columbia River Basin (the shared case) and toward their own homelands. Where is moisture coming from? Where is it going? With no internet, use a printed, dated screen capture labeled SNAPSHOT: [PLACEHOLDER: instructor's dated capture].
3. **From weather to climate:** show one day of temperature at the local station (see Local lesson), then 30 years of the same date. Ask: what did one day tell us? What did 30 years tell us? The 30-year view works on paper as a printed table or a dot plot drawn by hand.

## Check for Understanding

1. In your own words, what is the difference between weather and climate?
   Listen for: weather is what the air is doing now or today; climate is the pattern over a long time, about 30 years or more.
2. Draw or tell what happened inside the bottle when the cloud appeared.
   Listen for: the pressure dropped, the air cooled, and water vapor gathered on tiny particles into droplets we can see.
3. What did 30 years of the same date tell us that one day could not?
   Listen for: a pattern or a trend, how much one day can vary, or whether that date is getting warmer or cooler over time.
