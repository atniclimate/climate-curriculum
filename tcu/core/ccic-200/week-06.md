## Key lesson

Projections are not predictions of a single future. They are the physics of the climate system run forward under different choices, and they are only as useful as our ability to read them honestly.

## Key takeaway

Data literacy is sovereignty. A Nation that can read its own climate data can decide for itself what matters, instead of waiting for someone else to tell it.

## Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

- Identify major climate data sources (station records, gauges, snow monitoring sites, satellites, global climate models) and read a climate record.
- Describe Indigenous Knowledges as historical climate information, with appropriate care.
- Explain emissions scenarios and downscaling in plain language.
- Read and explain labeled charts for {{place.climate_unit}}, for the shared case, and for their own homelands.

## Hands-on activity

**Trend Lab.** Every view in this lab is a dated SNAPSHOT from the case pack or an instructor capture, printed as well as projected. Each view shows its label (SNAPSHOT or TEACHING MODEL), its source, its capture date, and, for any projection, the scenario, the period, the baseline, and the words "projection, not a prediction." A blank is unknown, never zero.

1. **Read a record (Shared case: {{case.shared}}).** As a class, read one long temperature record for a station in the shared case, from the shared case pack `[PLACEHOLDER: shared-case station record, SNAPSHOT]`. What is the baseline? What is the trend? What is year-to-year noise? Hand twin: on the printed chart, students draw the trend line by hand and circle one noisy year.
2. **This place.** From the climate profile (Local lesson), each student reads one variable for {{place.climate_unit}}: the historical baseline and a mid-century projection under a lower and a higher emissions scenario, as the case pack names them. Students from other homelands may ask the instructor ahead of class for the same view of home `[PLACEHOLDER: SNAPSHOT views of students' own homelands, captured by the instructor on request]`. Hand twin: copy the two scenario values into a two-row table and say the difference aloud.
3. **Current conditions.** Look at the instructor's SNAPSHOT of current snowpack, streamflow, and drought, first for the shared case, then for {{institution.state}}. Ask: how does this year sit beside the long-term picture?
4. **Visualization critique.** Students trade charts and ask: Is the axis labeled? Are the units clear? Are the source and date shown? Is the label (LIVE, SNAPSHOT, or TEACHING MODEL) there? Does a projection say "projection, not a prediction"? Is a blank shown as unknown, not zero? What would mislead someone?
5. **Knowledge as record.** Discuss how community observations, harvest timing, and oral histories can extend or check the instrumental record, and why such knowledge is shared only with permission and stays under the community's control. No student is asked to share such knowledge in class.

## Check for Understanding

1. What is the difference between a projection and a prediction? Say it, or draw it as two paths from today.
   Listen for: a projection is "if we choose this, then that"; it runs under a scenario and is not one fixed future.
2. On a printed SNAPSHOT chart, point to the baseline, the trend, and one year of noise.
   Listen for: the baseline is the reference period; the trend is the long direction; noise is the year-to-year bounce around it.
3. Why do the lower and higher scenarios end up in different places?
   Listen for: choices about emissions that people are still making; the difference is not fate.
