# The Sovereignty Primer (CCIC 200)

Climate work is sovereignty work. Deciding what matters, what data to trust, who holds knowledge, and who decides: these are the questions a Nation answers as a government. This primer runs through CCIC 200 as a thread and lives here as one page. It moves outward in three rings, like circles on water: the Nation, Nations acting together, and the world.

How to use it: read the ring a week points to, then come back to "Questions to carry." Learners analyze and quote public words. A learner may read and quote a Nation's or an organization's own public words, short and cited. No learner speaks or acts as a Nation, a council, an Elder, or a knowledge holder, and nothing on this page speaks for any of them.

Every date and article number below carries a [verify] tag until it is checked on the issuer's own page.

## Ring 1: The Nation

**Sovereignty.** A Tribal Nation is a government. It governs its lands, its citizens, and its affairs, and it was doing so long before any state or country was drawn on a map. Here, the Nation(s) whose homelands the campus is on: [NEEDS TRIBAL CONFIRMATION: the Nation(s) whose homelands the campus is on].

**Reserved rights.** When Nations signed Treaties, they kept, or reserved, rights they already held: to fish, hunt, gather, and live on and with their lands and waters. The Treaties did not give those rights to the Tribes; the Tribes kept them. The U.S. Supreme Court said so in *United States v. Winans* (1905). So we say "reserved rights," not "granted rights."

**The trust responsibility.** The United States made promises in Treaties and in law, and it carries a duty to keep them: to protect Tribal lands, the lands and assets held in trust for Nations, and the right of Nations to govern themselves [verify]. When climate change harms Treaty foods, waters, and homelands, the trust responsibility is part of the conversation.

**Self-determination.** A Nation decides its own future and runs its own programs. In climate work that means a Nation writes its own climate plan, sets its own priorities, chooses its own partners, and decides what its data may be used for.

**Why it matters for climate.** A Nation that can read its own climate data, name its own relatives and values, and write its own plan is acting as a sovereign. Outside data and tools support those decisions; they never replace them.

What a student does with Ring 1: name who decides for any concern; read a Nation's published plan or statement in its own words; never fill in what only the Nation can say.

## Ring 2: InterTribal

**Nations acting together.** Nations often work together when a problem is bigger than any one of them: a river shared by many Nations, a fish that travels through all their waters, a federal policy that touches every Tribe.

**How they do it.**

- **InterTribal organizations.** There are national organizations, such as the National Congress of American Indians [verify], and regional ones. In this region: {{region.intertribal}}. In the shared case, {{case.shared}}, Nations work together through bodies such as the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC) and the Affiliated Tribes of Northwest Indians (ATNI).
- **Resolutions.** Many InterTribal organizations adopt resolutions: written positions their member Nations agree on, often about climate, water, energy, or consultation [verify]. A resolution is that organization's own voice.
- **Consultation.** Federal agencies are expected to consult with Tribal governments, government to government, before actions that affect them (Executive Order 13175, 2000) [verify]. Nations sometimes prepare for consultation together and share what they ask for.
- **Shared positions.** Nations may speak together at hearings, in comment letters, and at international meetings, each still speaking as itself.

**Care.** An organization's words belong to that organization. Students read and quote them, short and cited, with the date; students do not summarize them as if speaking for the organization, and nothing here is said on any organization's behalf.

What a student does with Ring 2: find the InterTribal organizations active in {{region.name}} from their own public pages; read one public resolution or position; say what it asks for and who it is addressed to.

## Ring 3: International

**United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.** Adopted by the UN General Assembly in 2007 [verify]; the United States announced its support in 2010 [verify]. It speaks to self-determination, to lands and waters, to Indigenous Knowledge, and to free, prior, and informed consent before projects that affect Indigenous lands (for example, Article 32) [verify]. It is a statement of rights that Nations may use in their own advocacy; it does not speak for any Nation.

**The UN climate convention.** The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), adopted in 1992 [verify], is where countries meet each year to work on climate change. Its **Local Communities and Indigenous Peoples Platform** was set up through decisions of the convention's parties beginning in 2015 [verify], as a place for Indigenous Peoples to share knowledge and take part in the convention's work.

**The Paris Agreement.** Adopted in 2015 [verify]. It says adaptation should be guided by the best available science and, as appropriate, by the knowledge of Indigenous Peoples (Article 7, paragraph 5) [verify].

**The IPCC and Indigenous Knowledge.** The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) assesses climate research for the world's governments. Its recent assessment reports recognize Indigenous Knowledge as a source of understanding about climate change and adaptation [verify]. Recognition in a report is not permission: knowledge stays under the authority of the people who hold it.

**The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance.** Published by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (2019) [verify]; see Carroll, S. R., et al. (2020), *Data Science Journal* 19. Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, and Ethics. CARE gives broad principles. OCAP® (First Nations Information Governance Centre) is a First Nations framework from Canada, not a general rule for U.S. Tribes. Local Contexts labels are created and applied by Indigenous communities themselves, never by a course or a project.

What a student does with Ring 3: find where an international text names Indigenous Peoples; quote it short and cited; ask how a Nation might, if it chose, use those words in its own work.

## Putting the rings to work

CCIC 200 builds understanding. TCR 201 puts the rings to work. Four kinds of action carry students across:

1. **Policy watch.** Follow the policies, programs, and rules that touch a concern: federal, state, InterTribal, and international. Date every status, because policy changes fast.
2. **Consultation.** Learn how government-to-government consultation is supposed to work, what a Nation can ask for, and how to tell meaningful consultation from a notice.
3. **Resolutions.** Read InterTribal resolutions in their own words: what they ask for, who they ask, and what changed after.
4. **Coordination.** Map who an action would touch: the Nation, neighboring Nations, InterTribal bodies, counties, states, agencies, and partners. Climate work crosses every one of these lines.

In all four, students work as analysts, planners, and staff who write questions and drafts. The Nation decides.
